OVERVIEW: WHO IS PROTECTED

As of 2024–2025: 22 states and 23+ municipalities have active salary history ban laws. These laws prohibit employers from asking about your current or past compensation during the hiring process.

A federal agency-level ban (for federal civilian jobs) took effect October 2024.

===

STATES WITH STATEWIDE SALARY HISTORY BANS (18 States)

California:
- Employers cannot ask about or use salary history
- 15+ employees must post pay scales in job postings

Colorado:
- All employers banned, including remote positions based in other states
- All job postings must include wage range AND benefits

Connecticut:
- Cannot ask unless voluntarily disclosed
- Must provide pay range upon request before offer

Delaware:
- Cannot ask or use salary history
- Must disclose range after initial interview

District of Columbia:
- Prohibited across entire hiring process
- Must include pay range in postings + disclose healthcare benefits

Hawaii:
- Cannot inquire about compensation history
- 50+ employees must list salary range

Illinois:
- Prohibits salary history questions; bans retaliation for refusing to disclose
- 15+ employees must post pay scales (effective January 1, 2025)

Maine:
- Banned until after conditional offer is made
- Must provide pay range upon request

Maryland:
- Cannot ask about or use previous pay
- All employers must disclose ranges and benefits (effective October 1, 2024)

Massachusetts:
- Cannot seek prior compensation
- 25+ employees must post pay ranges (effective October 29, 2025)

Minnesota:
- Salary history questions prohibited
- 30+ employees must include salary ranges in postings (effective January 1, 2025)

Nevada:
- Cannot seek or use compensation history
- Must provide range after interview

New Jersey:
- Cannot inquire during hiring process
- Must provide range upon request after candidacy determination

New York:
- Prohibits questions and any use of salary history
- 4+ employees must include ranges in all job postings

Oregon:
- Cannot ask about salary history
- Must provide range upon request in final hiring stages

Rhode Island:
- Questions banned
- Must disclose pay range upon applicant request before compensation discussion

Vermont:
- Cannot inquire about prior pay
- Must disclose range upon request (not required to post in job listings)

Washington:
- Questions prohibited; also covers internal promotions
- 15+ employees must post pay ranges in job postings

===

STATES WITHOUT STATEWIDE PROTECTION

Alabama, Michigan, North Carolina, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Texas, Utah, Wisconsin have no statewide salary history ban.

Notable local exceptions:
- Cincinnati and Toledo (Ohio): ban applies to employers with 15+ employees
- Philadelphia (Pennsylvania): local ordinance despite no state law (check current status)

Michigan and Wisconsin: state laws actually PROHIBIT local governments from passing salary history bans.

===

WHAT IS PROHIBITED UNDER THESE LAWS

Employers in ban states cannot:
1. Ask "What is your current salary?" or "What were you making at your last job?"
2. Request a compensation history disclosure on applications
3. Ask about total compensation packages, bonuses, or benefits from prior roles
4. Contact previous employers to verify or obtain salary information
5. Use a third party (recruiter, background checker, staffing agency) to obtain salary history
6. Screen candidates based on salary history
7. Consider previous pay when determining job offers or comp decisions

===

WHAT CANDIDATES CAN DO IF ASKED IN A BAN STATE

Recommended response in ban states:
"I prefer not to share my salary history, but I'm happy to discuss my compensation expectations for this role."

Protective actions to take if asked anyway:
- Document the date, time, interviewer name, and exact wording of the question
- Take a screenshot or copy any written requests for salary history
- Note any follow-up behavior (sudden loss of interest, ghosting) that suggests retaliation
- Gather witness information if others heard the question

Legal remedies if the law is violated:
- File a complaint with your state's labor department
- Deadlines to file range from 6 months to 3 years depending on state
- Private right to sue available in: California, Colorado, Illinois, Massachusetts, New York, Washington
- Remedies can include back pay, wage differentials, attorney's fees, civil penalties of $500–$25,000

===

EXCEPTIONS TO THE BAN

Even in ban states, the following are typically allowed:
1. VOLUNTARY DISCLOSURE: You can share your salary history if you bring it up without being prompted
2. PUBLIC RECORD: Government employees' salaries that are already in public record may be referenced
3. VERIFICATION ONLY: If you voluntarily disclosed a number, they may verify its accuracy
4. COLLECTIVE BARGAINING: Union/CBA situations may have separate rules
5. INTERNAL PROMOTIONS: Some states' bans only cover new hire situations, not internal comp changes

===

REMOTE WORKERS: WHICH STATE'S LAW APPLIES?

Key principle: If you live and work in a ban state, you're covered — even if the employer is headquartered elsewhere.

Colorado explicitly covers out-of-state companies with any Colorado-based employee.
California's law applies to remote workers located in California.

Practical implication: If you're a remote worker in New York and interviewing with a company headquartered in Texas, New York's salary history ban applies to your interview process.

===

PAY TRANSPARENCY LAWS: A RELATED TREND

Many states with salary history bans also require pay transparency (posting salary ranges in job listings). This works in your favor:
- If a company is required to post the range, you know what they're budgeting
- "I see the range is $150K–$200K. Given my experience, I'm targeting the upper end of that range" is a more powerful opening than guessing

States with pay transparency posting requirements (as of 2025): California, Colorado, Connecticut, DC, Hawaii, Illinois (15+), Maryland, Massachusetts (25+, Oct 2025), Minnesota (30+), Nevada, New York, Rhode Island, Washington.

===

WHAT TO DO IF YOU'VE ALREADY DISCLOSED YOUR SALARY

If you disclosed your salary (in a state where they can legally ask, or before you knew better):

1. In subsequent conversations, shift to future expectations: "Now that we've discussed the role in more detail, I'm focused on [target range] for the offer."

2. Reframe early in the process: "I gave you a rough number earlier, but having learned more about this role, I think [higher number] is the right comp target."

3. If a ban-state violation occurred: document it, and decide whether to escalate (you may have leverage in a negotiation — the company violated state law and you can note that, carefully).

4. If the number was lower than market: address it head-on. "I know I shared [X] as my current comp, but market data for this role shows the range is [Y–Z]. I'm targeting the market rate."

The lesson: Never disclose current salary before an offer is made. It can only anchor the negotiation downward.
