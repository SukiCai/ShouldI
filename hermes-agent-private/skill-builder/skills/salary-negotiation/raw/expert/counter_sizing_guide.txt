THE EMPIRICAL BASELINE: WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS WHEN YOU COUNTER

Research across ~3,858 tech job seekers (2023–2025):
- Average increase from countering: 12.45% (~$27,000/year over initial offer)
- Overall negotiation success rate: 66% get what they ask for
- Offer withdrawal rate: only 6% (94% of offers remain intact after a counter)
- Average increase for those who negotiate: 18.83% from original offer

The fear of losing the offer is statistically unjustified. Employers expect candidates to negotiate — withdrawal is rare and almost always reserved for candidates who are aggressive or disrespectful in tone, not those who ask for more.

2025 market context: Tech salary growth has slowed to 1.6% (vs 2.9% in 2024, 3.5% in 2023). Counter offer effectiveness remains high even as baseline growth has moderated.

HOW MUCH TO COUNTER: BENCHMARKS BY SCENARIO

Standard range: Counter 10–20% above the initial offer.

The right counter amount depends on:
1. How far the offer is from market (levels.fyi anchor)
2. Your competing offer leverage (BATNA)
3. Whether you have exploding-offer pressure
4. Company stage (startup vs. public company)

SCENARIO A: Single offer, no competing offer, market-rate initial offer
→ Counter 10–12% above initial offer
→ This leaves room for the company to "meet in the middle" at ~5–8% above initial
→ Your psychological anchor gets the company above their opening without triggering pushback

SCENARIO B: Single offer, below-market initial (>15% below levels.fyi median)
→ Counter 20–25% above initial offer
→ Justify with specific market data: "levels.fyi shows median for this level at [X] — I'd need to be at [Y] to align with market"
→ Don't frame it as "I need more" — frame it as "here's what the market looks like"

SCENARIO C: Competing offer in hand
→ Counter to your target number, not 10–20% above initial
→ The competing offer is your anchor, not the initial offer
→ Script: "I have another offer at [X]. I'd prefer to work here — can you match [X]?"
→ You don't need to disclose the competing company's name, but the number is your leverage

SCENARIO D: Counter-offer from current employer (retention scenario)
→ Most retention counter-offers match external offer exactly or exceed by 5–10%
→ Key question: does this solve the underlying reason you considered leaving, or just the money?
→ Research shows 80% of people who accept counter-offers leave within 12 months anyway

===

WHAT TO ANCHOR ON: THE COUNTER NUMBER STRUCTURE

Start from market, not from initial offer:
- Pull levels.fyi for the specific company, level, and location
- Your counter should land at or slightly above the levels.fyi median for that role
- This frames your ask as "market alignment" not "I want more"

Anchor above your target:
- If your target is $200K total comp, counter at $215K
- This gives the recruiter a "win" by negotiating you down to your actual target
- Recruiters are trained to move toward the middle — use this against them

For base vs. total comp:
- Counter on base first, then equity separately
- "I'd need base to be at $190K" — don't muddy it with equity math in the first exchange
- After base is settled, re-open equity: "The equity grant — is that negotiable as well?"

===

WHEN TO ANCHOR HIGH VS. WHEN TO BE PRECISE

Anchor HIGH (give a round number above target) when:
- You have leverage (competing offers, multiple rounds of interviews, obvious fit)
- The initial offer is significantly below market
- You're early in the negotiation (first counter exchange)

Be PRECISE (give an exact number) when:
- You're in round 2+ of negotiation and both sides have narrowed
- You have a specific market data point to justify the number
- You're close to closing and want to signal seriousness

The psychology: precise numbers ("$192,400") signal that you've done research. Round numbers ("$200K") signal you're anchoring. Use each at the right moment.

===

HOW MANY ROUNDS TO EXPECT

Typical negotiation sequence:
- Round 1: You counter (10–20% above initial) → company responds with ~40–60% movement toward your ask
- Round 2: If needed, you respond to their counter → narrow further
- Round 3: Closing — one side says "this is our best" or "we can do X and consider this closed"

Most tech company negotiations resolve in 1–2 rounds on salary. Equity and signing bonus may have separate tracks.

When to stop:
- If company says "this is our final offer" and it meets 90%+ of your target, accept
- "Best and final" often has 5–10% more room on signing bonus even when base is locked
- Continuing to push after "final" without new leverage risks damaging the relationship

===

THE SIGNING BONUS PIVOT (WHEN BASE IS LOCKED)

If the company says base is fixed:
- Signing bonus is almost always still negotiable — it doesn't affect their headcount budget
- "If the base can't move, could you increase the signing bonus to [X]? That would help bridge the gap."
- Target: enough signing bonus to cover your first-year comp gap
- Signing bonuses of $20K–$80K are standard at large tech companies for senior roles

If equity is the lever instead:
- "Could the equity grant be increased to [X shares]?"
- Equity is often more flexible than base at growth-stage companies
- Note: equity vests over time; signing bonus is immediate — they serve different needs

===

THE FORMULA: COUNTER = BASE TARGET + 10% BUFFER + SPECIFIC JUSTIFICATION

Example:
- Initial offer: $180K base + $80K equity/yr + $15K signing
- levels.fyi median for this level: $220K total comp
- Your counter: "I'd need base to be at $195K and equity at $120K/yr to align with market compensation for this level. I have levels.fyi data showing median total comp at $220K for equivalent roles."
- You don't counter signing bonus in the same breath — save it as a later lever if base/equity don't fully close the gap.
