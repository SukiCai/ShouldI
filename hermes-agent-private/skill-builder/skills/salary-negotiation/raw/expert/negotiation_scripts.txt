WHY SCRIPTS MATTER

Most people know they should negotiate but don't know the exact words. Paralysis in the moment leads to either not asking or asking poorly. These scripts are starting points — use them, then adapt to tone and context.

Key principle: "Never give a number first. Always negotiate after you have the offer. Always be enthusiastic in tone, precise in ask."

===

SCRIPT 1: DEFLECTING SALARY EXPECTATION QUESTIONS PRE-OFFER

Situation: Recruiter asks "What's your current salary?" or "What are your salary expectations?"

Response (in states with salary history bans):
"I prefer not to share my current compensation — I want to focus on what's right for this role specifically. What's the budgeted range for this position?"

Response (states where you can be asked):
"I'd rather not anchor the conversation too early. I'm more focused on whether the role is the right fit. Can you share the range you're targeting for this level?"

If they push:
"I understand you need to know if we're in the right range. Let me put it this way: if the compensation is competitive for this level in [city], I'm sure we can find common ground. What does your budget look like?"

Why this works: You're not stonewalling — you're redirecting toward their number, which is almost always more useful than anchoring with yours.

===

SCRIPT 2: RECEIVING AN OFFER — BUY TIME WITHOUT REJECTING

Situation: Recruiter just gave you a verbal offer.

Response:
"Thank you so much — I'm really excited about this role and the team. This is helpful to have in writing. Once I receive the formal offer letter, I'll review everything and come back to you within [24–48 hours]. Is that timing okay?"

Why: Never accept or reject on the spot. You need time to compare to market data, and saying "I'll review it" is not a negotiation — it just buys time without creating friction.

===

SCRIPT 3: OPENING A COUNTER (SALARY FOCUS)

Situation: You have the offer letter and want to counter on base.

Response (phone call preferred):
"Thank you for the offer — I'm really excited about this opportunity and I can see myself doing great work here. I've done some research on compensation for this level using levels.fyi, and I was hoping we could discuss the base salary. Based on market data, I was expecting something closer to [target number]. Is there flexibility there?"

Key elements:
- Express genuine enthusiasm FIRST (this is not a tactic — it signals good faith)
- Name the market data source
- Give a specific number (not a range — ranges anchor at the low end)
- End with a question, not a statement

Variation with competing offer:
"I have to be honest — I'm in a difficult position. I've received another offer at [competing amount] from [company/or 'another company']. I'd prefer to work here, but I need to make a financially responsible decision. Is there any room to get closer to [competing amount]?"

===

SCRIPT 4: REQUESTING A PHONE CALL INSTEAD OF EMAIL

Situation: Recruiter is handling everything over email. You want to negotiate live.

Response (email):
"Before I respond with any specific thoughts on the offer, would it be possible to find 15 minutes to talk? I find it's easier to have this conversation by phone. Would [time] or [time] work for you?"

Why: Recruiters batch email negotiations and can easily say no. On a call, they're advocating for you in real time. The live conversation is where movement happens.

===

SCRIPT 5: RESPONDING TO "THIS IS OUR BEST AND FINAL"

Situation: Company says they've done all they can do.

Response A (if you want to accept):
"I appreciate you being direct. I understand this is the limit. I'm still very interested in joining — can we finalize the paperwork? I did want to ask one more time about the signing bonus specifically — is that a completely separate lever, or is that also at ceiling?"

Response B (if you need more movement):
"I hear you — I appreciate the transparency. I want to be on your team, and I'm trying to make this work. If base is truly at the ceiling, is there room to increase the signing bonus by [amount]? That would help me say yes today."

Response C (if you have competing offer leverage):
"I understand. I want to be straightforward with you — I have an offer at [X] that I need to respond to [timeline]. I'd prefer your company, but I need the numbers to be closer. Is there truly nothing else that can be done?"

Why "signing bonus" is always worth asking about: It comes from a different budget, doesn't affect payroll, and companies often have signing bonus flexibility even when salary bands are locked.

===

SCRIPT 6: NEGOTIATING AN EXPLODING OFFER EXTENSION

Situation: "You have until [tomorrow / Friday] to decide."

Response:
"I'm very interested in this role, and I want to make a confident, thoughtful decision rather than a rushed one. Would it be possible to extend the deadline by [one week / until [specific date]]? I want to make sure I'm saying yes for the right reasons, which benefits both of us."

If they push back:
"I understand the pressure. If you absolutely need an answer by [date], I can give you one — but I'd like to be upfront that a rushed decision is more likely to result in a 'no' than a thoughtful one. Can we find a middle ground?"

Why this works: Most exploding offers are negotiable. The company has invested 10–20 hours in interviewing you — they don't want to lose you over a 3-day extension. Invoking your desire to make a good decision is harder to refuse than "I need more time."

===

SCRIPT 7: THE SIGNING BONUS PIVOT (WHEN BASE IS LOCKED)

Situation: HR has confirmed the base salary cannot go higher due to band constraints.

Response:
"I appreciate you being direct about the salary band. I understand — I've seen that at other companies too. If the base truly can't move, could we look at a higher signing bonus to bridge the gap? A one-time payment of [amount] would help me feel good about the total package."

Follow-up if they agree in principle:
"That's great. Can we put that in the offer letter before I sign?"

Key: Get it in writing. "We'll figure out the bonus" is not a commitment.

===

SCRIPT 8: NEGOTIATING A NON-CASH LEVER (REMOTE WORK EXAMPLE)

Situation: You need full remote work; offer doesn't specify.

Response:
"I noticed the offer doesn't specify work location. I'm currently fully remote / I need to be in [city] — would this role support remote work arrangements? If so, can we include that in the offer letter?"

For hybrid situations:
"The offer mentions a hybrid arrangement. To plan my life around this, could we specify the expected in-office days per week explicitly? I want to make sure we're aligned before I sign."

===

SCRIPT 9: NEGOTIATING INTERNAL PROMOTION COMP

Situation: Manager just told you you're being promoted.

Response (say this before expressing full excitement):
"That's great to hear — thank you. Before we finalize anything, I'd like to talk through the compensation for the new role. What does the comp adjustment look like?"

If they give a number you want to counter:
"I appreciate the increase. I've looked at market data for [new title] and it looks like the range is [X–Y]. Can we get closer to [target]?"

If they say comp discussions happen after the formal promotion:
"I'd actually prefer to align on comp before accepting the new role — I want to make sure we're starting the next chapter on the same page. Can we discuss that now?"

===

SCRIPT 10: CLOSING THE NEGOTIATION

Situation: You've gotten close to your number and want to close.

Response:
"I think we're very close. If you can get to [final number], I'm ready to sign today. I'm genuinely excited about this and I want to move forward."

Why: Offers to sign "today" create urgency on their side that works in your favor. It also signals you're negotiating in good faith (not stringing them along).

===

GENERAL PRINCIPLES FOR ALL SCRIPTS

1. TONE: Enthusiastic, collaborative, not adversarial. You're both trying to reach an agreement.
2. SPECIFICITY: Give exact numbers. "Something closer to $200K" is less effective than "I'm looking for $195K."
3. SILENCE: After giving your number, stop talking. The discomfort of silence is the recruiter's problem, not yours.
4. PHONE FIRST: Negotiate on the phone, confirm in writing. Email negotiations lose nuance and allow easy "no."
5. ONE ASK AT A TIME: Don't counter on five things at once. Prioritize and sequence.
