THE FUNDAMENTAL ASYMMETRY

Internal promotion negotiations and external offer negotiations operate under completely different power dynamics:

EXTERNAL NEGOTIATION:
- You have maximal information asymmetry in your favor — they don't know your reservation price
- You can credibly threaten to walk (they've invested heavily in interviewing you)
- Market comp data (levels.fyi, competing offers) provides objective anchors
- They're paying a premium to recruit — signing bonus, recruiter fees, ramp time

INTERNAL PROMOTION NEGOTIATION:
- They know your current salary and roughly your reservation price
- You're "comfortable" — the status quo is the default
- You're often locked in mid-cycle (can't realistically interview externally immediately)
- Salary bands constrain what HR can approve, even if manager wants to pay more

The implication: Internal promotion negotiations have much less leverage unless you have an external offer in hand.

===

THE EXTERNAL OFFER AS INTERNAL LEVERAGE

The single most effective tool in internal promotion negotiation is a real, competitive external offer. Without one:
- You're negotiating from a position of comfort, not credibility
- Manager can delay, promise, or partially deliver without consequences
- "We'll make it right at your next review" is a common deflection

With an external offer:
- The leverage is immediate and concrete
- Managers have clear cost of inaction (losing you + replacing you costs 50–150% of your salary)
- HR often has emergency budget for retention that doesn't exist for standard promotions

How to present the external offer: "I'm in a difficult position — I received an offer that I'm seriously considering. I'd prefer to stay here, and I wanted to give you the chance to respond before I make any decisions." Then state the number explicitly.

Research shows: 80% of people who accept retention counter-offers still leave within 12 months. Don't accept a retention counter-offer unless the underlying reason for considering a move is genuinely resolved — not just compensated.

===

INTERNAL PROMOTION DYNAMICS WITHOUT AN EXTERNAL OFFER

When you don't have outside leverage, the strategy shifts:

1. Make the business case explicit:
   - Don't just ask for more — build the case for why the new scope/role warrants it
   - "I'll be taking on [specific responsibilities] that are at a different level than my current role"
   - Frame as scope justification, not personal entitlement

2. Anchor on the market rate for the new role (not your current salary):
   - "I researched comp for this role — levels.fyi shows [X] for equivalent scope. Can we get close to that?"
   - Internal hires are often offered below market on the assumption they're "already paid" — push back on this

3. Use the timing advantage:
   - Internal candidates often get paid below market because they don't negotiate
   - "I'm glad to hear you're considering me — before we finalize, I'd like to discuss compensation for the new level"
   - Say this before accepting the promotion, not after

4. Negotiate the promo criteria first, then comp:
   - If the promotion is discretionary, nail down what "getting promoted" looks like in writing
   - A verbal promise of promotion is worth nothing; criteria and timeline in writing is worth something

===

WHEN THE COMPANY MAKES A RETENTION OFFER

Signs your company is willing to counter:
- Manager proactively asks "what would it take to keep you?"
- HR reaches out quickly after hearing you're considering leaving
- They match or beat the external offer without much negotiation

The key question to ask yourself: "If this job paid exactly what they're now offering, would I have stayed?" If the answer is no — if the reason you looked was scope, manager, team, learning opportunity — a counter-offer won't fix that.

What companies know but don't say: They often make retention counter-offers knowing you'll probably leave anyway. The counter-offer buys them 6–12 months to find a replacement.

When to accept a retention offer:
- The external offer was primarily about comp (not scope or career trajectory)
- The company is willing to make structural changes you wanted (new manager, team, project)
- You genuinely prefer to stay and the delta was only money

When to decline a retention offer:
- You were pushed to look by factors beyond comp (bad manager, ceiling, stagnation)
- The counter-offer doesn't address the root cause
- You've now revealed to your company that you're looking, which changes the relationship

===

INTERNAL TRANSFER MECHANICS (TEAM SWITCH WITHOUT PROMOTION)

Internal transfers are a separate negotiation situation:
- Promo clock reset: moving internally often resets your promotion eligibility timer
- Network cost: you lose your current team's institutional knowledge of your work
- Leverage asymmetry: you have less leverage than an external hire for the same role

Best practice for internal transfers:
- Negotiate comp increase at the time of transfer, not "once you've proven yourself in the new role"
- "I'd expect the new role to come with a comp increase that reflects the scope change"
- Get transfer terms in writing from both current and new manager (especially around bonus proration and review timing)
- Understand whether the transfer affects unvested equity or bonus eligibility

===

THE PROMO CLOCK PROBLEM

At most large tech companies:
- After you receive a promotion, you're ineligible for another promotion for 12–18 months (promo clock)
- An internal transfer may start the clock over
- External new hire with the same level has the clock starting from their join date

Implication: If you're 10 months into a promo cycle and someone offers an internal transfer at the same level, you're effectively pushing your next promotion 2+ years away (10 months elapsed + 12–18 month new clock). This is a hidden compensation cost that most people don't calculate.

===

THE MARKET CORRECTION PLAY

If you're significantly underpaid internally vs. market:
- Don't wait for the annual review cycle
- Build the case and request a "market correction" discussion explicitly
- Most companies have a mechanism for off-cycle adjustments if the gap is documented
- "I've seen recent market data showing my comp is 20% below median for this role — I'd like to have a conversation about a market correction"

This works best when:
- You have clear market data (levels.fyi, job offers you've received)
- Your performance is strong (no recent negative feedback)
- You're in a company that values retention (not a company in downsizing mode)
