THE STRUCTURE OF MULTI-ROUND NEGOTIATION

Most people think of salary negotiation as a single event. It's actually a structured process with predictable rounds. Understanding the arc prevents you from either stopping too early (leaving money on the table) or pushing too far (damaging the relationship).

TYPICAL THREE-ROUND ARC IN TECH:

Round 1 — Opening Counter
- You counter at 10–20% above initial offer
- Company responds with 40–60% movement toward your ask
- Example: You ask $200K; initial was $175K; they come back at $185K

Round 2 — Narrowing
- You respond to their counter; narrow the gap further
- You may shift levers (if base feels locked, try signing bonus or equity)
- Example: You say "$190K is my target" → they offer $188K + $10K signing

Round 3 — Closing
- One party says "this is our best" or offers a final package
- The other accepts, declines, or makes one final ask
- This is where signing bonus / PTO / remote arrangements often get settled

Most negotiations resolve in 1–2 rounds. A third round is acceptable but risky — pushing to a fourth or fifth round signals you're maximizing extraction rather than finding mutual agreement.

===

WHEN TO STOP PUSHING

Signs you've reached the limit:
1. The company uses the phrase "our final offer" twice in the same conversation
2. The recruiter's tone shifts from collaborative to tense or administrative
3. The number they're quoting is already above or at levels.fyi median for the role
4. You've already moved on 3+ levers and they've matched or exceeded on all of them

When "final offer" has room:
- If they say "base is final" but haven't addressed signing bonus → signing is still live
- If the phrase came in an email → phone call still creates room to move
- If the recruiter seems apologetic or sympathetic → they may be advocating for you; one more ask might land

When "final offer" means final:
- The recruiter stops being apologetic and becomes matter-of-fact
- They give you a hard deadline with the final offer
- The number is at or above market median

===

BATNA: KNOW YOUR WALK-AWAY NUMBER BEFORE YOU START

BATNA = Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement. It's the number below which you walk away.

How to calculate your BATNA:
1. Your current comp (or best competing offer) → your actual alternative
2. The comp required to maintain your lifestyle/financial goals
3. The non-cash factors you'd need to compensate for any comp gap (remote work, scope, learning)

If your BATNA is your current job: be honest with yourself about whether you'd actually leave. If you wouldn't, you have weak leverage — and you should know that before negotiating.

If your BATNA is a competing offer: this is strong leverage. You can name the competing offer amount as your floor.

Never negotiate if you don't know your BATNA: going into the conversation without a walk-away number means you'll either accept something bad (no anchor) or overplay your hand (arbitrary anchor).

===

HOW TO RECOGNIZE WHEN YOU'VE EXTRACTED THE MAX

Signals:
1. Multiple rounds and they haven't moved in the last exchange
2. The recruiter volunteers "I've already gone back to the hiring manager twice" — they're signaling cost on their side
3. Total comp is now within 5% of your target number
4. You've moved levers from base to equity to signing bonus and they've responded to all three

At this point, continue negotiating has diminishing returns and real cost (relationship, goodwill, perception as a difficult employee before day one).

The closing move: "Thank you for going to bat for me on this. I think we're very close — if we can get to [final number], I'm ready to sign today." This closes the loop, creates urgency, and preserves goodwill.

===

SINGLE OFFER VS. COMPETING OFFER DYNAMICS

SINGLE OFFER (no competing offer):
- Your leverage is entirely about fit signaling and market data
- The company knows you don't have an alternative — they'll be firm
- Best strategy: anchor on market data, move levers, stop at 1–2 rounds
- Mistake to avoid: manufacturing fake competing offers (recruiters verify, and it destroys trust)

COMPETING OFFER:
- Strongest leverage in negotiation
- Share the number (you don't have to share the company)
- "I have another offer at $210K — I'd prefer your company, but I need to close the gap"
- Creates urgency: they now have a real decision deadline
- The company often goes back to their hiring committee; an emergency budget emerges

WHEN YOU GET A COMPETING OFFER AFTER ACCEPTING:
- Legally, you can back out before your start date (there's reputational cost, not legal liability)
- Use the competing offer to negotiate with your original company if you prefer them
- Or walk away — starting a relationship with a counter-offer situation is a judgment call

===

THE TIMELINE GAME

Companies control timing. Candidates often feel rushed. The tools:

Extension request: "Can I have until [date] to give you my decision?" — almost always yes if phrased as needing time to make a thoughtful decision.

Speed creation: "If you can get to [X], I can sign today." — creates urgency in the other direction and rewards movement with immediate resolution.

Competing offer deadline: "I need to respond to my other offer by [date]." — imports an external deadline that isn't yours to control.

Using all three strategically:
1. Get your extension
2. While evaluating, use the time to interview elsewhere and generate a competing offer
3. Use the competing offer or competing timeline to create urgency on your target company

===

POST-ACCEPTANCE NEGOTIATION (RARE BUT REAL)

After signing, most people think negotiation is over. It isn't:

At 90-day review: "I've gotten up to speed quickly. Based on what I've delivered in 90 days, I'd like to revisit the comp conversation."

If scope expands: "My responsibilities have expanded significantly beyond what was in the offer. I'd like to discuss whether that warrants a comp adjustment."

If market shifts significantly: "I've seen comp bands in my role move materially this year. Can we schedule a market correction conversation?"

These post-acceptance situations work only with strong performance and a track record that justifies the ask. They require the same data anchors and scripts as the original negotiation.
