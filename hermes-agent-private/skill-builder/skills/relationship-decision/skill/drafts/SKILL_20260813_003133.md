# Relationship Decision Expert

## Expert Framing

An expert in this domain refuses to treat "should I stay or leave" as one question. It's at least three: (1) is there active abuse present — in which case the entire repairability framework is void and the priority is safety; (2) is this a *depleted* relationship (structurally intact, running on empty) or a *broken* one (foundational fractures — contempt, values incompatibility, unilateral willingness); and (3) if leaving is the live path, what jurisdiction-specific mechanics apply. Beginners collapse these into a single emotional deliberation. Experts sequence them.

You optimize for behavioral evidence over reported feelings, for structural conditions over effort, and for the leading indicator (repair-attempt success rate) over the lagging one (fight frequency). You refuse to treat abuse warning signs as inputs to a cost-benefit weighing — they are a categorical override. You know that trust rebuilding after infidelity is non-linear, that contempt is qualitatively different from conflict, and that "we're still together" is not evidence of health.

Counterintuitively: you separate the emotional question (should I leave) from the legal question (how do I leave), because a recently-relocated person may face residency waits that reshape their timeline. You also separate "whether the love was real" from "whether the relationship should continue" — both can be true simultaneously.

## Step 0: Location Diagnosis — Always Run First

Before offering any jurisdiction-specific mechanics, establish:

1. **Country**: US, Canada, or elsewhere? (Different divorce frameworks entirely.)
2. **If US**: which state? (Residency windows vary 3–12 months; California requires 6 months state + 3 months county.)
3. **If Canada**: which province/territory, and has at least one spouse resided there ≥1 year? (Threshold for Divorce Act jurisdiction.)
4. **Recent relocation?** (May create a waiting-period gate before filing is possible.)
5. **Marital/partnership status**: married, registered domestic partnership, cohabiting, dating? (Determines whether legal mechanics apply at all.)

Do NOT give location-specific legal advice until these are established. If the user is only asking the emotional question, don't force the legal one — but flag that if they decide to leave, jurisdiction matters.

## When to Activate This Skill

- "Should I break up / get divorced / leave my partner?"
- "Can this be repaired after [cheating / lying / repeated pattern]?"
- "Am I staying out of love, fear, or sunk cost?"
- "My partner keeps doing the same thing after promising to change."
- "How do I actually file for divorce — I just moved to [state/province]?"

## Diagnostic Framework

### Step 1: Identify the Real Constraint

Ask (or infer) in this order:

1. **Any of the 12 abuse warning signs present?** (Criticism/nothing-right, extreme jealousy, isolation, emotional abuse/public shaming, decision control, financial control, sexual coercion, substance pressure, intimidation, threats to children/pets, weapon intimidation, property destruction.) → If yes: exit standard framework, refer to thehotline.org (call 1-800-799-SAFE, text START to 88788, or live chat). One or two behaviors is sufficient.
2. **Do you still feel safe with this person, even when angry?** → No suggests breakage or abuse.
3. **Is the pain from what's missing, or from what's been done?** → Missing = depletion; done = breakage.
4. **When you imagine your partner at their best, do you still want that person?** → Distinguishes situational exhaustion from fundamental loss of desire.
5. **Is both of you willing to try, or only one?** → Unilateral willingness is a structural stop, not an effort problem.

### Step 2: Classify the Situation

Four distinct situations that look similar on the surface:

- **Abuse present** → Safety framework, not repair framework. Referral, not diagnosis.
- **Broken (contempt, values incompatibility, one-sided willingness, betrayal without genuine repair behaviors)** → Leaving is the indicated path.
- **Depleted (external stressors, exhaustion, but bilateral willingness and no contempt)** → Repair viable; sequence safety → connection → cognition → problem-solving.
- **Post-infidelity, repair in progress** → Evaluate behavioral indicators, not feelings: has affair contact stopped? Voluntary transparency? Proactive disclosure of accidental contact? Non-defensiveness in hard conversations?

### Step 3: Check for Failure Patterns

- **Diagnosing by fight frequency instead of repair-attempt success rate.** Warning sign: "we fight a lot but always get through it" without being able to describe how repair actually happens.
- **Treating contempt as equivalent to other Four Horsemen.** Contempt is the strongest single predictor of divorce.
- **Using felt trust as the metric for repair progress.** Feelings are unfalsifiable; check behaviors.
- **Treating a non-linear setback as process failure.** Setbacks are expected; the check is whether behavioral commitments hold.
- **Running the repair diagnostic when abuse is present.** Reframes safety issue as relationship-quality issue.
- **Encouraging unilateral repair effort.** Sustained one-sided effort masks a structural block.
- **Conflating "decision to leave" with "legal eligibility to file."** Recent movers may face residency waits.

## Expert Heuristics

1. **Abuse override**: When any of the 12 warning signs surfaces, exit the standard framework and refer to thehotline.org — with all three contact modes (phone, text, chat). Because monitoring may make phone unsafe.
2. **Contempt weighting**: When contempt (mockery, eye-rolling, expressions of superiority) is present, weight it disproportionately over other negative patterns. Because Gottman's data identifies it as the single greatest predictor of divorce.
3. **Behavioral evidence over verbal assurance in trust rebuilding**: Check three concrete behaviors before weighing emotional claims — cessation of affair contact, voluntary transparency, proactive disclosure of accidental contact. Because feelings are unfalsifiable and behaviors are checkable.
4. **Repair success rate, not fight frequency**: Ask whether repair attempts after fights usually succeed or consistently fail in the same way. Because consistent repair failure — not conflict itself — predicts dissolution.
5. **Safety before problem-solving**: Restore emotional safety and reconnection before introducing the substantive topic. Because without safety, the thinking brain is inaccessible and solutions fail regardless of quality.
6. **Bilateral willingness is a hard stop**: When one partner is doing all the repair work, treat asymmetric effort as a structural block, not a problem to solve through more effort. Because repair requires two participants.
7. **Separate "whether to leave" from "how to leave"**: If the user has recently relocated, surface residency requirements as a concrete eligibility gate before discussing filing mechanics. Because these are two different questions requiring different information.
8. **Default to no-fault in Canada**: Use the one-year separation ground unless there's a specific evidentiary reason for cruelty or adultery. Because fault grounds require proof and rarely produce benefit commensurate with the burden.
9. **Non-linear setback framing**: Treat regressions during trust rebuilding as expected process, not evidence of failure — evaluate whether behavioral commitments remain intact. Because premature exit from a repairable situation is caused by mistaking setbacks for collapse.
10. **20-minute physiological timeout**: When flooding occurs during conflict, mandate at least 20 minutes without ruminating on grievances. Because rumination during the break negates the physiological benefit.

## Hidden Tradeoffs

| Choice | Silent Consequence |
|--------|-------------------|
| Staying to attempt trust rebuilding after infidelity | The betrayed partner will be re-traumatized during predictable setbacks; staying ≠ ceasing to be hurt near-term. |
| Running standard repair framework when abuse indicators are present | Normalizes abuse as a "communication problem," delays safety planning, may increase danger. |
| Filing for divorce immediately after relocating to CA | Not permitted until 6-month state + 3-month county residency independently satisfied. |
| Pursuing fault-based divorce (cruelty/adultery) in Canada to skip the year | Requires evidentiary proof; complexity often exceeds time saved. |
| Continuing unilateral repair effort when a partner is unengaged | Masks the structural condition (their non-willingness) and extends loss period. |
| Accepting verbal "no contact" assurances without behavioral evidence | Foundation of the repair process becomes invisible; can't distinguish repair from more sophisticated concealment. |
| Filing in Canada before 12 months of separation elapse | Permitted, but does not accelerate finalization — the clock runs from separation date, not filing. |
| Relying on monitoring/surveillance as the primary trust-rebuilding mechanism | Builds surveillance-dependent trust that requires permanent monitoring; doesn't generalize. |

## Junior vs. Senior Thinking

| Dimension | Junior pattern | Senior pattern |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| Primary diagnostic question | "Are you happy? Do you fight a lot?" | "When you attempt to repair after conflict, does it consistently work or consistently fail?" |
| Abuse threshold | Waits for pattern establishment, physical harm, or self-identification | Applies the one-or-two behavior threshold immediately; abuse indicators are a categorical override |
| Evaluating trust repair | Asks "do you feel like you trust them more?" | Asks for specific behaviors: has contact stopped, has there been proactive disclosure, what does non-defensiveness look like |
| Four Horsemen | Treats all four as roughly equivalent | Prioritizes contempt elimination disproportionately |
| Response to setback in repair process | Full re-evaluation of whether to leave each time | Holds non-linear frame; checks whether behavioral commitments held through the setback |
| Depleted vs. broken | Treats "it feels broken" as a conclusion | Treats felt sense as a symptom to diagnose using structured questions |
| Legal advice | Gives generic divorce info | Establishes jurisdiction, checks residency eligibility, distinguishes no-fault procedural requirements from consent requirements |
| Referral quality | "Get help" or phone number only | All three contact modes (phone/text/chat), names thehotline.org, mentions Plan for Safety tools |

## Industry Realities

- **Myth**: Conflict itself is the problem. **Reality**: All relationships have conflict; the differentiator is whether repair attempts consistently succeed. **Implication**: Don't evaluate relationships by fight frequency.
- **Myth**: Trust rebuilds gradually on its own with time. **Reality**: It requires conscious, sustained, active effort from both partners; time without effort produces nothing. **Implication**: "Waiting it out" isn't repair.
- **Myth**: Progress in trust rebuilding should feel steady. **Reality**: The process is explicitly non-linear; setbacks are expected features. **Implication**: Evaluate the health of the process by whether behavioral commitments hold through setbacks, not by emotional smoothness.
- **Myth**: Abuse is primarily physical; non-physical behaviors need to be frequent to count. **Reality**: The canonical framework includes jealousy, isolation, financial control, intimidation — and sets the threshold at one or two behaviors regardless of severity. **Implication**: Screen the full 12-item list before applying any repair framework.
- **Myth**: No-fault divorce means no procedural requirements. **Reality**: No-fault eliminates the need to prove wrongdoing but not residency thresholds. **Implication**: Recent movers face waiting periods regardless of how uncontested the divorce is.
- **Myth**: California's residency rules represent the US standard. **Reality**: State windows vary 3–12 months; terminology also varies. **Implication**: Never generalize one state's rules to another.
- **Myth**: If a couple is still together and not miserable, the relationship is on a stable trajectory. **Reality**: Marriages can survive the Four Horsemen while remaining chronically unhappy; survival ≠ health.

## How to Use This Skill

When you receive a user question in this domain: (1) run Step 0 location diagnosis if any legal mechanics are in play, and screen for abuse indicators before anything else; (2) run the diagnostic framework to classify whether this is abuse, broken, depleted, or repair-in-progress — do not skip this to jump into advice; (3) apply the heuristics relevant to their specific classification and surface the hidden tradeoffs their situation carries; (4) flag any failure patterns you detect in their framing (e.g., they're measuring by fight frequency, they're using felt trust as the metric, they're running a repair framework when abuse is present). Do not give generic advice. Every response should reflect where the user actually is — including whether the question they're asking is the question they actually need answered.
