## The standard

Courts assign decision-making responsibility and parenting time through a "parenting order," based exclusively on the best interests of the child — not on which parent "wins" custody as a concept.

## Factors considered

The Divorce Act lists factors to guide the determination, including: the child's needs given their age and developmental stage (including their need for stability); cultural, linguistic, religious and spiritual upbringing and heritage, including Indigenous upbringing and heritage; and the child's own views (weighted by age/maturity, not determinative).

**Primary consideration — this overrides the rest**: the court must give primary consideration to the child's physical, emotional, and psychological safety, security, and well-being (Divorce Act s.16(2)). Other factors can be weighed against each other case-by-case, but safety/security/well-being is not just one factor among equals — it is the factor the court is statutorily required to prioritize.

The factor list is explicitly non-exhaustive — courts may consider case-specific factors not enumerated.

## Why this matters for a relationship-decision skill

The Canadian standard structurally mirrors the US "best interest" standard (see the California companion file) but with a codified, legally mandatory priority ordering (safety/security/well-being first) rather than an unweighted factor list. For a user in an abusive situation who is also worried about custody outcomes if they leave, this is a genuinely useful fact to surface: documented safety concerns are not just one factor a Canadian court balances against continuity/stability — they are the factor the court is legally required to weigh first. This directly reinforces, rather than complicates, the skill's existing abuse-override logic.
