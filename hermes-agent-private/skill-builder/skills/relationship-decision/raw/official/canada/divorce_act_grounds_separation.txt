## Three grounds for divorce under the Divorce Act

1. Separation — spouses have separated and lived apart for at least one year
2. Cruelty — the spouse has been physically or mentally cruel, making it intolerable to continue living together
3. Adultery — the spouse has committed adultery

In practice, separation is by far the most commonly used ground (cruelty and adultery require proof and are rarely pursued given the one-year separation route exists as a no-fault path).

## Separation period requirement

12 months must elapse from the date of separation before a divorce based on separation can be granted — but the application can be *filed* before the 12 months is complete; it just cannot be finalized until the period elapses.

## Residency requirement

At least one spouse must have resided in a Canadian province or territory for at least one year before applying for divorce. If neither spouse resides in Canada, divorce under the federal Divorce Act is unavailable (limited alternatives may exist under the Civil Marriage Act for specific cases).

## Terminology shift (as of March 1, 2021)

The Act now uses "parenting arrangements" instead of "custody and access" to describe where children will live and how decisions about them are made — reflects a shift away from framing children as something one parent "wins."

## Why this matters for a relationship-decision skill

The 1-year separation-before-finalization rule (vs. the shorter, state-variable US residency windows) is a structurally different timeline. A user weighing "should I leave" under a Canadian marriage is implicitly weighing a longer minimum legal runway than a US equivalent — this should inform the "what's actually reversible on what timeline" dimension of the decision, not just the emotional one.
