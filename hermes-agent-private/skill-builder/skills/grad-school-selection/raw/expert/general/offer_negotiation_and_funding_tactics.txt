Source: Compiled from PhD admissions advisors, graduate student unions, and practitioner guides on offer negotiation
Topic: PhD offer negotiation — what is actually negotiable, when to ask, and how to use competing offers
Country scope: United States (primary), Canada (partial)

---

## The Core Insight: More Is Negotiable Than Applicants Realize

Most PhD applicants treat the offer letter as fixed. It isn't. Stipend amounts, summer funding, start dates, research budget, and conference travel are all frequently negotiated — and departments expect it from strong candidates. The mistake is assuming the first number is the final number.

The leverage window is narrow: after you receive an offer and before you accept it. Once you accept, negotiation is effectively over.

---

## What Is Actually Negotiable

### Stipend Amount

Stipend floors are often set by department policy, but the ceiling is not. In fields with external funding (STEM with active grants), advisors can supplement the department baseline from their grant budget.

How to ask:
"I'm very interested in joining your group. I've received another offer at [amount], and I wanted to ask whether there's any flexibility in the stipend for this position before I make my decision."

What works: a competing offer with a higher number. You don't need to reveal which program — just that you have a competing offer at a higher stipend.

What doesn't work: asking for more without a concrete competing offer or rationale.

Realistic outcome: $2,000–$5,000/year additional in STEM fields where RA funding is from advisor grants. Less flexibility in TA-funded programs where stipend is fixed by union contract.

### Summer Funding

The 9-month vs. 12-month distinction matters enormously but often isn't stated clearly in the offer. "Stipend" in many programs means 9 months — you are expected to find your own summer funding (RA, TA, fellowship, or go unfunded).

Questions to ask:
- "Is the stipend guaranteed for 12 months, or does it cover 9 months of the academic year?"
- "How do students typically fund their summers? Is summer RA support available from your grant?"
- "Have any students received summer fellowships through the department?"

Why this matters: a $34K/9-month stipend is effectively $28K/year annualized. A competing offer with a lower headline number but 12-month guarantee can be worth more.

### Conference Travel Budget

Most PhD programs have some conference travel support, but amounts vary enormously ($500–$5,000/year) and are rarely disclosed in offer letters. Ask:

"What's the typical conference travel support available for students in your group? Is there a department fund, or does it come from your research budget?"

Strong programs support 1–2 major conferences per year. Programs where students self-fund conference travel create a significant inequity that compounds over 5 years.

### Start Date

Most programs default to August/September. Deferring one semester or starting in January is often possible and can be valuable for international students who need time to arrange visas, or for students who want to complete a research project.

How to ask: directly. "Would it be possible to start in January instead of August? I'm finishing [project/commitment] and it would allow me to hit the ground running."

Success rate: moderate. Labs with immediate grant funding needs may push back.

### Coursework Waivers and Credit for Prior Work

If you have prior graduate coursework or have already completed equivalent training, many programs will waive specific requirements on request. This can shorten your time-to-degree.

How to ask: during the visit, to the DGS (Director of Graduate Studies), not to your prospective advisor. "I completed graduate-level coursework in X at my previous institution — do you have a process for evaluating transfer credit or course waivers?"

## How to Use Competing Offers as Leverage

The most effective negotiation position is a real competing offer with a specific number. Vague "I have other options" doesn't work. "I have an offer from Program X at $36,000/year with 12-month summer support and I'm trying to make my decision" works.

Ethics note: only use competing offers that are real and that you are genuinely considering. Using a fabricated offer is dishonest and can backfire (programs in the same field talk to each other).

Timing: do not mention competing offers in your first email after receiving the offer. Express genuine enthusiasm first. Then, in a follow-up call or email: "I wanted to be transparent — I have another offer I'm weighing at [amount]. Before I make my decision, I wanted to ask whether there's any flexibility on [specific item]."

## What Is Not Negotiable

- Union-negotiated TA stipends (these are fixed by collective agreement, not by the department)
- Base tuition remission (this is almost always a binary: full waiver vs. partial vs. none)
- Health insurance coverage tier (though which dependents are covered sometimes varies)
- Department-wide course requirements

---

## Dual-Offer Leverage Tactics

If you hold two strong offers:
1. Tell both programs you have a competing offer (you can without revealing specifics)
2. Ask each what they can do to make their offer more competitive
3. Give a realistic timeline ("I need to decide by [date] — is there anything you can share about flexibility by then?")

The goal is not to play programs against each other in bad faith — it's to ensure you're getting the best actual offer before committing to 5 years of work.

## Field-Specific Variation

**STEM (lab sciences, CS, engineering)**:
- Most funding is RA from advisor grants — more flexible on stipend
- Summer funding is often available but must be asked for
- Conference travel budgets tend to be higher

**Social sciences**:
- More mixed funding (TA + fellowship + RA)
- Department-level funding guarantees are more common
- Fellowship competitions (NSF GRFP, Ford) are important and worth asking your advisor to support your application

**Humanities**:
- Funding often comes from department stipends + teaching
- Less flexibility on stipend amount (often flat rate across cohort)
- Fellowship opportunities (Mellon, ACLS) exist but are post-PhD more than during
- The negotiation that matters most: which years require teaching, and how much

**Professional programs (MBA, MPP, MFA)**:
- Funding is often merit scholarships, not stipends
- Explicitly negotiable against competing admits — programs do this routinely
- "I have X offer from Program Y, what can you do?" is completely standard

## The Pre-Acceptance Visit: What to Observe

The visit weekend is not just a social event — it's due diligence. What to look for:

1. Do current PhD students seem able to speak freely, or do they look over their shoulders when talking to you?
2. How do students describe their relationship with their advisor — specific and positive, or vague and deflecting?
3. Ask students who are 3+ years in: "Are you on track for your expected graduation timeline?" If most aren't, that's structural.
4. Observe the physical space: is there equipment? Are students around? Ghost labs (empty, unused) are a signal.

The best questions to ask current students during the visit:
- "What's one thing you wish you'd known before accepting?"
- "What do you wish was different about the program?"
- "How responsive is your advisor when you need feedback?"
- "Have you ever had a funding gap or scare during your PhD?"
