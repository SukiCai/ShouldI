## Why Humanities PhDs Work Differently

Most generic PhD advice is written from a STEM/lab-science perspective: advisor-first logic, grant-funded RA positions, 5-year funded programs. In humanities fields (literature, history, philosophy, art history, religion, classics, etc.), the structure is fundamentally different. Applying STEM logic to a humanities application produces systematically wrong advice.

---

## Funding Structure Differences

**STEM:** Funding flows from advisor's research grants → RA positions → advisor has discretion over who gets funded.

**Humanities:** Funding is typically allocated by the department as a cohort package — all admitted students receive the same fellowship/stipend regardless of advisor. The advisor has little or no control over your funding.

**Implications:**
- In humanities, the question "does your advisor have an active grant?" is irrelevant to your funding
- The relevant question is: "What does the department guarantee for all PhD students, and for how many years?"
- Humanities stipends come from: departmental fellowships, external fellowships (Mellon, ACLS, Woodrow Wilson), and teaching assistantships — not PI grants

**Teaching requirement:**
Humanities PhD students typically teach more than STEM students. A common structure: years 1–2 fellowship (no teaching), years 3–5 TA positions requiring 1–2 sections per semester. Teaching is real work — 10–20 hours/week that must come from somewhere, typically extending time-to-degree.

The negotiation that matters in humanities: which years require teaching, how many sections, and whether there's a dissertation writing fellowship year without teaching obligations in years 5–6.

## Time-to-Degree Differences

Humanities PhDs take longer — significantly longer — than STEM PhDs.

Median time-to-degree in humanities (NSF Survey of Earned Doctorates, recent years):
- English/Literature: 9.2 years
- History: 9.5 years
- Philosophy: 8.4 years
- Foreign languages: 9.0 years

Compare to STEM medians:
- Computer science: 5.7 years
- Biology: 5.8 years
- Engineering: 5.3 years

**Why it matters for funding:** A 5-year funding package in humanities covers about 55% of the typical time-to-degree. Years 6–9+ must be funded through: dissertation fellowships (competitive), adjunct teaching (poorly paid), external grants, or personal savings. This is not exceptional — it is the norm.

**Questions to ask:**
- "What percentage of students in recent cohorts finished within the guaranteed funding window?"
- "What does the department do for students in years 6–8 who are still writing their dissertation?"
- "How many students currently hold external dissertation fellowships (Mellon, ACLS)?"
- "Does the department have a completion fellowship for ABD students close to finishing?"

## The Humanities Academic Job Market: Realistic Assessment

The humanities academic job market is structurally different from STEM and has deteriorated significantly over the past two decades:

**Supply/demand imbalance:**
- English PhD programs produce approximately 1,000 new PhDs per year
- Tenure-track job openings in English: approximately 200–400 per year (declining)
- Placement rate into tenure-track positions from even top programs: 30–50%

**Program prestige matters more in humanities:**
- Top-10 programs dominate academic job placement in humanities fields
- Unlike STEM where strong publications can overcome program rank, humanities hiring committees routinely filter by institutional affiliation at early stages
- The advisor's network and letter-writing relationships with hiring committees are critical

**Geographic constraint:**
- Academic job searches typically require applying to 50–150 positions nationally and internationally
- Accepting a tenure-track position requires willingness to relocate anywhere
- This constraint is especially acute for international students whose immigration options are tied to institutional location

**Alternative career paths:**
Many humanities PhDs end up in: publishing, non-profit administration, policy work, higher education administration, think tanks, journalism. These paths are not failures — but they are worth modeling explicitly before a 9-year commitment.

## Advisor Role in Humanities: Different, Not Less Important

The advisor relationship in humanities differs structurally from STEM:

**What the advisor does:**
- Guides your dissertation conceptually (most important role)
- Writes letters for fellowships, conference grants, and job applications (critical)
- Provides connections to hiring committees and job market intelligence
- Advocates for you within the department and field

**What the advisor does NOT do (unlike STEM):**
- Control your funding (department controls this)
- Determine your research topic (you propose your own project)
- Provide you with a lab, equipment, or existing data

**Advisor-specific evaluation in humanities:**
- Publication record in your subfield (does their work speak to your project?)
- Track record of student job placement (not just graduation — academic job placement specifically)
- Reading speed (how long do they take to return chapter drafts? Months = problematic)
- How many current students do they have? Advisors who take too many students at once often cannot read manuscripts promptly
- Are they still actively engaged with the field or have they moved to administration?

**Red flags specific to humanities advisors:**
- Takes more than 3 months to return a dissertation chapter draft
- Has not placed any students into tenure-track positions in the past 5 years (even from a mid-tier program, this should happen occasionally)
- Is in a senior administrative role (chair, dean) that reduces engagement with students
- Specialization is tangential to your project (prestige of advisor means less if their work doesn't speak to yours)

## Program Evaluation: Humanities-Specific Checklist

Unlike STEM, where you evaluate advisor first and program second, in humanities you must evaluate both with equal weight:

**Program-level questions:**
- What is the stipend amount and how many years is it guaranteed?
- What are the teaching obligations in each year?
- What is the department's actual job placement rate (not "many students go on to academic careers" — ask for specific numbers)?
- Does the department have a dedicated job placement director or advisor?
- What external fellowship support do students typically receive (Mellon, ACLS, Woodrow Wilson, FLAS for area studies)?
- What is the average time-to-degree for students who finish?

**Advisor-level questions:**
- How many students have you placed into tenure-track or visiting positions in the past 5 years?
- How long do you typically take to return chapter drafts?
- How many students are you currently advising? Are you taking new students this year?
- Are you planning to remain active in your research program over the next 8–10 years? (Asking about sabbatical or administrative plans is reasonable)

## Canada-Specific Differences

Canadian humanities PhD programs differ in key ways:

- SSHRC (Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council) provides competitive doctoral fellowships ($20,000–$35,000 CAD/year) that students apply for and bring to their program — this can supplement department funding significantly
- SSHRC Doctoral Fellowships are portable: you can hold them at any eligible Canadian university, giving you flexibility
- Program lengths are shorter on paper (4 years expected) but actual completion times are similar to the US
- Tenure-track market is similarly constrained but a smaller total number of positions

Canadian humanities PhDs have better immigration outcomes in some respects: the Post-Graduation Work Permit (PGWP) applies to PhD graduates, providing 3 years of open work authorization that can lead to permanent residence through Express Entry.
