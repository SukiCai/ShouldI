## Part 1: National Academies Report on PhD Mentorship (2019)

"The Science of Effective Mentorship in STEMM" (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2019) is the most comprehensive research synthesis on what makes mentorship work or fail in STEM graduate education.

### Seven Core Findings

**Finding 1: Mentorship directly affects career outcomes**
Graduate students with positive mentoring relationships publish more research and persist longer in their fields. Effective mentorship involves receiving BOTH career support (technical guidance, sponsorship, networking) AND psychosocial support (emotional encouragement, role modeling, validation).

**Finding 2: Underrepresented students are most affected by mentorship quality**
Underrepresented students leave STEMM fields at higher rates than peers, partly due to lack of effective mentorship and identity conflicts with academic culture. The gap is structural, not individual.

**Finding 3: Mentorship requires intentional design**
"Effective mentorship involves building interpersonal trust" and "evolves through different stages." Passive advising (advisor available when needed) is insufficient for most students. Effective mentorship requires structured engagement, regular goal-setting discussions, and reflection.

**Finding 4: Mentorship is a learned skill, not a personality trait**
"Mentorship education programs demonstrably improve mentor effectiveness." This means: a great researcher is not automatically a great advisor, and an advisor who was never trained in mentorship may be unintentionally harmful despite good intentions.

**Finding 5: Multiple mentors outperform single-mentor models**
PhD students benefit from multiple mentoring relationships — co-advisors, peer groups, external mentors outside the lab. Programs that structure mentorship as one-advisor-only create a single point of failure.

**Finding 6: Identity integration matters for completion**
Mentors who acknowledge and support students' multiple identities (national origin, gender, first-generation status) produce better retention outcomes.

**Finding 7: Institutional accountability is absent in most programs**
Widespread adoption of effective mentorship requires systemic shifts in rewards, culture, and accountability structures. Most institutions do not formally evaluate advisor mentorship quality.

---

### What Negative Mentorship Looks Like (from the Report)

- **Absent or unavailable**: Physical absence, no scheduled meetings, only available in emergencies
- **Unrealistic expectations without guidance**: "Figure it out" culture without concrete support
- **Credit-taking**: Taking first authorship on student work, presenting student ideas without attribution
- **Inappropriate delegation**: Using PhD students as lab managers, administrators, or personal assistants
- **Mentor self-absorption**: Lab structured around mentor's needs (grants, reputation) rather than student development
- **Lack of expertise**: Advisor whose own research has gone stagnant or whose grants have expired
- **Interpersonal dysfunction**: Volatile mood, favoritism, inconsistent feedback

**Critical for application**: None of these failure modes are visible in a faculty member's publication list or university ranking. They are only detectable by talking to current and former students.

---

### National Academies Recommendations for Graduate Students (Practical Application)

**Rec 1**: Seek advisors who define mentorship as "a professional, working alliance in which individuals work together over time to support personal and professional growth through career AND psychosocial support." Advisors who only discuss research and publications are providing career support without psychosocial support.

**Rec 5**: Pursue multiple mentoring relationships — not just your primary advisor. Seek out co-advisors, thesis committee members who are actively engaged, peer mentors in more senior years, and professionals outside academia.

**Rec 7**: If experiencing negative mentorship, neutral third-party resources (ombudspersons, graduate student support offices) are the appropriate first contact — not confronting the advisor directly, which creates retaliation risk.

## Part 2: PhD Completion and Attrition Rates

### Council of Graduate Schools (CGS) PhD Completion Project

The largest systematic study of PhD completion in the US (29 major US and Canadian research universities; funded by Ford Foundation and Pfizer):

- **57%** of doctoral candidates completed their degree within 10 years
- **20%** completed within 7 years (i.e., "on time" for most programs)
- Under the most favorable conditions: no more than 75% ever complete

**Implication**: 25-43% of students who begin PhD programs never finish. This is not a small minority — it is structural.

### Completion Rates by Institution (selective examples)

| Institution | Completion rate | Within timeframe |
|------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Stanford | 63.2% | Within 6 years |
| Cornell | 81% | Within 7-12 years |
| University of Chicago | 73% | (2013-2014 cohort data) |
| UCLA | ~80% | Average 5 years |
| Arizona State | ~85% | Within 6 years |

Institution-level variation is significant. The same field at a better-resourced institution can have 20-30 percentage point higher completion rates.

### Time to Degree

- National average: **7.3 years** for doctoral completion
- Humanities and social sciences: **8.4-9.5 years** median (confirmed by AHA and APA data)
- STEM (sciences, engineering): **5-6 years** typical
- Computer science: typically **4.5-6 years**

**The 5-year-funding vs. 7-year-completion gap**: Most PhD funding packages guarantee 4-5 years of support. Median completion time is 5-7 years. The gap is where funding crises occur.

### What Predicts Completion vs. Attrition (CGS findings)

**Factors associated with COMPLETION:**
1. Financial support (stipend continuity throughout program)
2. Mentoring/advising quality
3. Social environment and peer support
4. Program quality and structure

**Factors associated with ATTRITION:**
- Funding interruptions or uncertainty
- Advisor relationship problems (most commonly cited reason for leaving)
- Isolation from peer community
- Time-to-degree exceeding 7 years (attrition increases sharply after year 7)

**Attrition timing**: Most attrition happens at two points: (1) after comprehensive exams (students realize research is not what they expected), and (2) years 5-7 (funding ends, dissertation stalls).

### Women and Underrepresented Minorities

CGS data: "Women and underrepresented minorities had higher late completion rates than men and white students" — meaning they take longer on average, not that they fail to complete. But longer time-to-degree creates greater financial exposure and higher probability of funding gaps.

## Decision Framework: Using These Findings to Evaluate a Program

Before accepting a PhD offer, ask:

1. **"What is the 10-year completion rate for your PhD program?"** — Below 60% should prompt concern
2. **"What happens to students after their funded period ends?"** — Reveals whether the program has a plan or a funding cliff
3. **"Can I speak with a PhD student in year 4 or 5 in your lab specifically?"** — Year 4-5 students are in the most vulnerable period; their experience is the most predictive
4. **"Does the program have an ombudsperson or student advocate for advisor relationship issues?"** — Programs with no formal recourse concentrate power entirely in the advisor
5. **"What is the average time to degree for recent graduates in this lab?"** — Advisor-specific time-to-degree varies enormously
