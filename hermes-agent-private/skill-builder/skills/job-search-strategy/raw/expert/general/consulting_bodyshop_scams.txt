Country scope: United States (primary)

## What Body Shops Are

"Body shops" (also called "consulting body shops," "staffing firms," or "bench consultants") are staffing companies that:
1. Hire international students with H-1B or other work visas
2. Place them at client companies for project work
3. Pay the international worker a salary (often lower than market) while billing the client at a higher rate
4. Keep the margin

Many legitimate staffing firms exist and operate legally. The problem is a specific category of fraudulent or non-compliant body shops that target international students who are desperate for visa sponsorship and may not know the rules.

---

## The Specific Fraud Pattern

**Step 1**: The body shop offers to sponsor the international student for H-1B. This seems like a solution to the student's visa problem.

**Step 2**: The offer letter shows employment at the staffing firm with a salary. However, the work will actually be done at a client company (not the staffing firm).

**Step 3**: The H-1B petition is filed with USCIS using the staffing firm as the employer. USCIS requires the petition to specify the place of employment. For staffing arrangements, this is legally possible but must be disclosed correctly.

**Step 4**: Problems begin:
- The "salary" in the offer may not actually be paid during "bench" periods (when between client placements)
- The client placement may fall through, leaving the worker in limbo
- The worker may be pressured to work without pay while waiting for placement
- The fraudulent firm may have filed the H-1B petition with incorrect information
- The firm may charge the worker for legal fees (illegal) or immigration costs (illegal)

**Outcome**: The international student may be out of money, in an incorrect immigration status (if the H-1B was filed incorrectly), and with limited legal recourse.

## Specific Red Flags — Each One Is a Stop Signal

### Red Flag 1: They ask you to pay for H-1B fees
Under INA § 212(n), employers must pay H-1B filing fees. Workers cannot be charged for:
- I-129 petition fee ($460)
- ACWIA Training Fee ($750 or $1,500)
- Premium processing fee ($2,805)
- Attorney fees for the H-1B petition

If a "sponsor" asks you to pay any of these fees, this is illegal and a scam indicator. Stop the process immediately.

### Red Flag 2: They offer H-1B sponsorship without knowing what you'll do
Legitimate H-1B sponsorship requires a specific job with a specific SOC code, specific wage level, and a specific worksite. An employer who can't describe your actual job duties in detail before filing is either misrepresenting the role or filing fraudulently.

### Red Flag 3: "Bench" period with no pay or reduced pay
H-1B regulations (20 CFR 655.731) require employers to pay the H-1B worker the offered wage for the entire period the H-1B petition is valid — even during periods of non-productive time. Paying you nothing while you "wait for a placement" is not only unfair, it's a violation of H-1B regulations.

### Red Flag 4: They tell you to "create a resume" with fake experience
Some fraudulent firms coach international students to misrepresent their experience on resumes for client placements. This is resume fraud. It can affect your immigration record and potentially result in removal proceedings if it affects the underlying H-1B petition.

### Red Flag 5: Extremely fast H-1B sponsorship offer to someone they barely know
Legitimate H-1B sponsors have a specific role in mind, have gone through internal approval processes, and have engaged immigration attorneys. An offer to "sponsor your H-1B" within days of a first conversation, without knowing what you'll actually do, is a signal.

### Red Flag 6: The company has no web presence, no verifiable clients, and employees who won't name clients
Legitimate consulting firms will tell you who their clients are (at least in general terms). A staffing firm that can't or won't name any actual clients may be operating on paper only.

### Red Flag 7: Contract with unusual terms (non-compete, liquidated damages for leaving)
Some body shops include contracts requiring you to stay for a specific period or pay a "penalty" for leaving. These clauses are often unenforceable but are used to pressure workers into staying even in exploitative conditions. Any contract with unusually large financial penalties for resignation is a red flag.

## How to Vet Small Employers and Staffing Firms

Before accepting any offer from a small employer or staffing firm that claims they'll sponsor H-1B:

**Verification step 1**: H-1Bdata.info or MyVisaJobs.com → search the company name → see how many H-1B petitions they've filed, in which occupations, and what their denial rate is. A company that has never filed an H-1B or has very high denial rates is a risk.

**Verification step 2**: Ask specifically: "How many current H-1B holders work at your company, and what are their roles?" A company that genuinely sponsors should be able to answer this immediately.

**Verification step 3**: Ask: "Which immigration law firm handles your H-1B petitions? How long have you worked with them?" Legitimate sponsors have established relationships with immigration attorneys.

**Verification step 4**: Check their USCIS employer data. USCIS publishes data on H-1B approvals by employer. If a company claims to be a regular sponsor but doesn't appear in USCIS data, that's inconsistent.

**Verification step 5**: Ask to speak with current H-1B employees at the company. A company that hesitates to connect you with satisfied employees may not have any.

## If You're Already in a Body Shop Arrangement

If you're currently working at or have a pending offer from a body shop that is behaving problematically:

### Option 1: Consult an independent immigration attorney
Do NOT use the attorney provided by the body shop — they represent the employer, not you. Seek an independent attorney for a consultation on your specific situation. Many immigration attorneys offer free or low-cost initial consultations for workers in compliance situations.

### Option 2: Contact the Department of Labor Wage and Hour Division
If you are not being paid the prevailing wage or are not being paid during bench periods, you can file a complaint with the DOL. H-1B wage violations are taken seriously and workers are protected from retaliation for filing complaints.

### Option 3: Understand your portability rights
Under the American Competitiveness in the 21st Century Act (AC21), if you've been employed in H-1B status for 180 days or more, your H-1B is portable — you can transfer to a new employer without starting over. This means you're not trapped even if you're in a bad arrangement.

### Option 4: Look for other sponsors immediately
Don't stay in a fraudulent arrangement longer than necessary because of fear. A new employer can file an H-1B transfer. Your status doesn't get canceled if you start this process quickly.

## The Opportunity Cost Framing

Many international students enter body shop arrangements because they feel they have no other option. The actual calculus:

**What body shops offer**: H-1B sponsorship + some income
**What legitimate alternatives offer**: OPT + time to find a real sponsor, OR cap-exempt employment (universities, nonprofits), OR job change

A student who spends 2 years in a body shop arrangement often:
- Has a resume gap or weak employment history (body shops often don't count for much in future hiring)
- Has had their OPT clock running without meaningful work experience accumulation
- Has potentially been in non-compliant immigration status (which can affect future visa applications)

The comparison isn't "body shop vs. nothing." It's "body shop vs. 12 more weeks of legitimate job searching + OPT extension for STEM."
