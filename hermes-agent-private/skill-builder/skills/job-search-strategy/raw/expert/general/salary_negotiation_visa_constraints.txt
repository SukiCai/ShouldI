Topic: Salary negotiation under visa constraints — what is safe to negotiate, what isn't, and how to reframe the fear
Country scope: United States (primary), Canada (partial)

## The Core Problem: Under-Negotiation from Fear

International students negotiate salary at significantly lower rates than domestic students. The most common reason: fear that negotiating will cause the employer to withdraw the offer, and without the offer they have no visa.

This fear is largely unfounded, and acting on it has real financial cost. An international student who accepts the first offer instead of negotiating loses an average of $5,000–$15,000 in first-year salary. Compounded over a career, the cost of under-negotiation from a first job is often $100,000+.

The reality: employers almost never withdraw offers because a candidate negotiated professionally. Withdrawing an offer over a salary negotiation would destroy the employer's reputation and create legal risk. It essentially never happens at legitimate employers.

---

## What Happens When You Don't Negotiate

Employers build negotiation room into first offers because they expect candidates to negotiate. The first offer is usually not the best offer. If you accept without negotiating, you simply don't get the money that was available.

More importantly: for H-1B purposes, the employer must pay the prevailing wage — the minimum salary that USCIS considers appropriate for the role/location. If an employer is sponsoring you for H-1B, your salary is already above a regulatory floor. Offering you less than that floor is not allowed.

This means: for sponsored roles, the employer's offer is already constrained from below. Negotiating upward from that floor is completely normal and expected.

## What Is Safe to Negotiate (US)

### Base salary
Always negotiable. Standard range: 5–20% above initial offer at tech companies. The ask is professional, expected, and almost never causes offer withdrawal.

How to ask: "I'm really excited about this opportunity and I'd like to make this work. The initial offer was $X — based on my research on market rates and the scope of the role, I was hoping we could discuss something closer to $Y. Is there flexibility?"

### Signing bonus
Often more flexible than base salary because it doesn't affect ongoing compensation benchmarks. If base salary negotiation hits a ceiling, signing bonus is often where movement happens.

"If the base is firm, would there be flexibility on a signing bonus? I have some relocation costs and it would help me make the transition."

### Equity (RSUs or options)
At tech companies with equity compensation: negotiate the number of shares/units, the cliff, and the vesting schedule. This is separate from base salary and often has more room.

### Start date
Fully negotiable. You can ask for 2–4 additional weeks if you need time for visa processing, relocation, or finishing your degree. Employers routinely accommodate this.

### Remote work / hybrid arrangement
Negotiable before joining, much harder after. If location flexibility matters for your situation (e.g., PGWP in a specific province, family considerations), this is the time to raise it.

### Professional development budget, conference allowances
At smaller companies without fixed policies, these are negotiable. "Does the role come with a professional development budget for conferences or certifications?"

## What Is Harder to Negotiate (US)

### The fact that they sponsor H-1B
If the employer offered you the job, they have already decided to sponsor. This is not negotiable — you can't negotiate them into better immigration support than they're offering.

### H-1B cap vs. cap-exempt status
You cannot negotiate an employer from cap-subject to cap-exempt. That's determined by their organizational structure, not their willingness to negotiate.

### Immigration attorney choice
Most employers use their own immigration attorneys for visa sponsorship. You typically cannot insist on using a different attorney.

### Benefits at large companies
Benefits (health insurance, 401k match, PTO) at companies with 1000+ employees are typically standardized. At small companies, more flexibility exists.

## The Timing Question: When Does Visa Status Come Up?

**The optimal timing**: Visa status comes up during the offer stage, not before. You handle it during negotiation as follows:

1. Express genuine enthusiasm for the offer: "I'm really excited about this role and I'd like to make this work."
2. If they haven't already asked, you disclose: "I want to make sure we're aligned on timeline — I'll need H-1B sponsorship starting [date]. I understand [Company] has sponsored H-1Bs before — is that process something we can confirm is in place?"
3. Then negotiate on salary: once visa sponsorship is confirmed, negotiate on compensation.

The mistake is to negotiate visa first, then salary. If you frame it as "will you sponsor me?", you're asking for a favor — which weakens your negotiating position. The better frame is confirming an operational detail, then negotiating as a professional.

**For PGWP holders in Canada**: visa status barely comes up because you have open work authorization. You can negotiate exactly as a domestic candidate would.

## Reframing for International Students

### The fear frame (wrong): "I should be grateful they're willing to sponsor me. I shouldn't push my luck by negotiating."

### The reality frame (correct): "I am a qualified professional who happens to need visa sponsorship. Visa sponsorship is a real cost to the employer, but so is a bad hire, or a gap in headcount, or a missed project deadline. They've already decided I'm worth hiring and worth sponsoring. The sponsorship decision is made. Now we're negotiating compensation."

The visa sponsorship decision and the salary decision are separate. Confirming that an employer will sponsor does not mean you've used up your negotiating capital.

## What To Do If An Employer Says "The Offer Is Fixed"

At large tech companies with standardized pay bands: this is often true. Your leverage is different.

Options:
1. Ask about signing bonus (often outside the pay band system)
2. Ask about level (sometimes you can negotiate the job level, which changes the band)
3. Ask about equity (often separate from base salary bands)
4. Use a competing offer: "I have another offer at $Y — is there any flexibility here?" A competing offer is the most powerful negotiating tool regardless of visa status.

### Using competing offers when visa-constrained

Some international students hesitate to use competing offers because they fear seeming demanding. This is a mistake. A legitimate competing offer is the clearest signal of market value. It never reads as demanding — it reads as in-demand.

"I've been fortunate to receive another offer at $Y, which has [specific thing this offer doesn't have]. [This company] is my first choice because [genuine reason]. Is there any flexibility to get closer to $Y?"

You do not have to disclose which company the competing offer is from, though you can if you want to.

## Canada: Salary Negotiation with PGWP

PGWP holders have the same negotiating position as domestic Canadian workers. There is no "sponsor" to worry about offending.

Standard Canadian tech negotiation norms:
- Offers are typically 5–15% below what's available
- Asking for 10–15% above initial offer is completely normal
- CAD vs. USD compensation: if comparing to US offers, remember CAD/USD exchange rate, plus Canada's universal healthcare (no employee premium) effectively adds $3,000–$8,000/year in compensation value

## The Script That Works

For US international students, this is a template for a professional salary negotiation conversation:

"Thank you so much for the offer — I'm genuinely excited about this role and the team. Before I respond formally, I wanted to have a conversation about the package. The base is $X. Based on my research on comparable roles in [location] and my specific background in [area], I was hoping we could discuss something closer to $Y. Is there flexibility on the base, or alternatively on the signing bonus or equity?"

Then: silence. Let them respond. Don't fill the silence with back-pedaling.

Most common response: "Let me check with [HR/manager] and get back to you."
Most common outcome: some increase, even if not the full ask.
