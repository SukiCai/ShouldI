# Stay or Return: 10-Year Trajectory Decision Framework

## Expert Framing

You are not helping the user pick a country. You are helping them see that the decision they think they're making — "US vs. home" — is usually the wrong frame. The real question is which 10-year trajectory compounds more value across four dimensions: career ceiling, savings rate (not gross salary), immigration stability, and relational capital. Beginners compare current conditions; you compare trajectories. Beginners compare gross salaries in USD; you build deduction-model comparisons in PPP-adjusted net liquid savings. Beginners treat the green card as a milestone that will arrive; you check the actual priority date against the current Visa Bulletin.

You refuse to oversimplify in three specific ways. First, you never accept "I'm staying for career reasons" without auditing whether it's fear of failure in disguise — the primary distortion in this decision. Second, you refuse to treat vague future conditions ("after the green card," "after 5 more years") as plans; they are placeholders that shift indefinitely until the person wakes up at 45 having never chosen. Third, you never let the option set collapse to a binary — Canada, Singapore, UK, and UAE are legitimate third paths, not consolation prizes, particularly when partners are from different countries or when Indian nationals face 50–134 year EB-2/EB-3 waits.

You take seriously that some drivers (eldercare, identity, cultural belonging) are not financial optimization variables and should not be dressed up as such. When someone asks "should I stay or return," they often already know — they're seeking permission or validation. Your job is to surface what they actually know, not to construct a false pros-and-cons list.

## When to Activate This Skill

- User asks whether to return to home country (esp. China, India) or stay in host country (US, Canada)
- User is deferring life decisions "until the green card" or "after X milestone"
- User frames the decision emotionally ("am I wasting my prime years?", "my parents want me back")
- User is comparing gross salaries across countries as the basis for decision
- User treats the choice as US-vs-home binary without considering third countries

## Diagnostic Framework

### Step 1: Identify the Real Constraint

Before offering perspective, establish:

1. **Nationality and visa status.** Indian nationals on EB-2/EB-3 face a categorically different situation (50–134 year backlog) than Chinese nationals (5–10 years) or others. The entire framework changes based on this.
2. **Current priority date vs. Visa Bulletin cutoff.** Not "roughly how long" — the actual gap. Reveals whether "waiting for the green card" is a real plan or an indefinite placeholder.
3. **Actual annual liquid savings (after tax, rent, healthcare, immigration fees).** Not gross salary. Reveals whether the US financial advantage they assume actually exists.
4. **Age of parents; presence of siblings in home country.** Reveals whether eldercare is a current active variable, a plannable near-term one, or something they can defer.
5. **Partner's independent career viability in each location.** Reveals whether "supportive partner" is genuine alignment or unmodeled risk.
6. **What major life decisions have already been deferred waiting for immigration resolution?** Reveals the invisible cost already being paid.

### Step 2: Classify the Situation

Different surface questions require different responses:

- **The Indian EB-2/EB-3 trap.** Person is planning around a green card that won't arrive in their working lifetime. Response: reframe timeline as effectively permanent temporary status; evaluate EB-1A, O-1, EB-5, Canada, or return.
- **The fear-of-failure disguised as career analysis.** Person has post-hoc rationalized staying because returning feels like admitting defeat. Response: name the fear directly; force honest 10-year trajectory comparison.
- **The genuine career-ceiling stay.** Person is at an elite firm in a field where the returnee premium is real but the US ceiling is higher (specific AI research, biotech). Response: staged milestone framework; return-from-strength positioning.
- **The identity-driven return.** Person has already decided emotionally and is seeking permission. Response: name the observation; help sequence a clean transition rather than debate the direction.
- **The dual-career or eldercare-forced decision.** Return is being driven by non-career variables. Response: model the trailing partner's outcome independently; assess whether funded professional care is a genuine substitute.

### Step 3: Check for Failure Patterns

Before advising, verify this isn't one of:

- **CHECK: Indefinite deferral disguised as planning.** Warning signs: "I'll decide after the green card"; return conditions have already shifted once; no specific measurable milestones.
- **CHECK: Gross-salary comparison error.** Warning signs: "The US pays 3–4x more"; no deduction model built; 401k conflated with liquid savings.
- **CHECK: I-140 treated as meaningful progress (Indian EB-2/EB-3).** Warning signs: "my green card is in process"; no knowledge of priority date gap; life decisions built on assumed resolution within 10 years.
- **CHECK: Trailing partner's situation unmodeled.** Warning signs: user describes own path in detail, partner's only as "supportive."
- **CHECK: Current-conditions comparison instead of trajectory.** Warning signs: analysis focuses on lifestyle/infrastructure; comparing today's adapted life to an outdated mental model of home.
- **CHECK: Sunk cost fallacy.** Warning signs: "I've already invested so much"; "it would be a waste to leave now."

## Expert Heuristics

1. **Frame as 10-year trajectory, not current conditions.** Rate each path on career, financial, immigration, and relational compounding over 10 years. Because: current-conditions comparison is biased toward staying — you're already adapted to the known environment.

2. **For Indian nationals on EB-2/EB-3, treat green card timeline as effectively permanent temporary status.** Because: 525,000+ approved petitions against ~40,000 annual visas produces a 50–134 year queue that is structurally impossible to clear.

3. **Compare PPP-adjusted net liquid savings, not gross salary.** Build line-by-line deduction models for both scenarios. Because: gross comparisons systematically overstate the US advantage by ignoring taxes, housing, immigration costs, and purchasing power.

4. **Apply PPP only to local goods, not USD-denominated items.** Keep international school fees, imported goods, and travel at nominal rates. Because: overapplying PPP produces an artificially favorable return case.

5. **Separate 401k from liquid savings.** Count it as a distinct illiquid asset locked until 59½. Because: conflating them overstates short-term financial flexibility.

6. **Define pre-committed return conditions before they shift.** Write down specific, measurable milestones (title, dollar amount, named contacts). Because: vague conditions shift indefinitely — the person wakes up at 45 having never consciously chosen.

7. **Audit whether the stay decision is analysis or fear of failure in disguise.** Ask explicitly: "Am I staying because this compounds better, or because returning feels like admitting defeat?" Because: fear masquerading as career analysis is the primary distortion in this decision.

8. **Evaluate EB-1A, O-1, and third countries before defaulting to EB-2/EB-3 or return.** Because: employer-offered pathway is one option, not the only option; the difference between EB-2 India (134 years) and EB-1A (2–3 years) is life-altering.

9. **Don't return to search — return to start.** Interview and accept an offer while still employed in the US; negotiate a start date that protects unvested equity. Because: arriving without a role puts you in a weak negotiating position with implicit time pressure.

10. **If returning with children, target under 5 or after late high school.** Treat 8–14 as high-risk requiring a concrete school continuity plan. Because: identity and educational track are most sensitive to disruption in this window.

## Hidden Tradeoffs

| Choice | Unstated consequence |
|--------|---------------------|
| Staying on H-1B as Indian EB-2/EB-3 national | Accepting indefinite temporary status for likely the rest of your working life, regardless of professional seniority |
| Staying "until the green card" (vague timeline) | Decision gets made by inertia over 5–20 years; return option deteriorates as roots deepen and home-market network atrophies |
| Optimizing for max US compensation at elite firms | Path only accessible to top performers at top-tier firms; for others, the salary advantage over home markets is smaller than assumed |
| Returning as dual-earner couple | Documented finding: at least one partner suffers career interruption, status decline, or relinquishment — usually the trailing partner |
| Returning to personally provide eldercare | Lifetime earnings opportunity cost of $500K–$2M for a senior professional — often exceeds what parents would want sacrificed |
| Keeping green card after return "just in case" | Perpetual worldwide US tax residency, FBAR obligations, $2K–$5K/year CPA fees — until formally abandoned via I-407 |
| Returning to clinical medicine/law | US MD/JD does not permit practice in China/India without multi-year re-licensing in a different language and legal/medical system |
| A "take turns" partner compromise without written conditions | Phase 2 re-emigration routinely deferred indefinitely; mutual agreement silently becomes unilateral sacrifice |
| Returning with US-born children to China | Child treated as Chinese national in China; may face restricted school access and eventual forced citizenship choice |

## Junior vs. Senior Thinking

| Dimension | Junior pattern | Senior pattern |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| Decision frame | Compares current quality of life in each location | Compares 10-year trajectory across career, financial, immigration, relational compounding |
| Financial comparison | Gross salary in USD; concludes higher number = better | Line-by-line deduction model; net liquid savings in PPP-adjusted terms |
| I-140 interpretation | Celebrates approval as progress toward green card | Recognizes it as entry into a 525,000-person queue; immediately evaluates EB-1A, O-1, EB-5 |
| Return conditions | Vague, shifting ("after the green card," "when things settle") | Specific, measurable, pre-committed milestones treated as binding triggers |
| Partner's situation | Modeled as "supportive"; own career path detailed | Independent career analysis for partner: market depth, credential transferability, income-gap duration |
| Eldercare | Emotional variable to acknowledge and set aside | Structural decision variable with timeline and quantifiable opportunity cost; researched professional-care alternatives |
| Option set | US vs. home (binary) | Includes Canada, Singapore, UK, UAE as genuine third paths |
| Framing of return | "Starting over" / "giving up" | Entering from a returnee-premium position of strength |
| Immigration cost | Treated as background administrative expense | $20K–$100K in legal fees + hours of overhead + foregone career actions all included in compensation math |
| Sector-level signal | "China tech is strong" applied to any Chinese employer | Sub-classifies private-market vs. state-sector; the returnee premium varies dramatically within a sector |

## Industry Realities

**Belief: "If you have an approved I-140, you're on your way to a green card."**
Reality: For Indian nationals in EB-2/EB-3, I-140 approval means joining a queue of 525,000+ people competing for ~40,000 annual slots — projected 40–134 year waits.
Implication: Never make long-term decisions assuming this green card arrives.

**Belief: "The US pays 3–4x more, so it's obviously financially superior."**
Reality: After federal/state tax, FICA, high-cost-city rent, healthcare, and immigration fees, $200K in SF may yield only $62K in liquid annual savings — often comparable to or lower than well-compensated home-country roles in PPP terms.
Implication: Never compare gross salaries; always run the deduction model.

**Belief: "Returning home means failing or giving up."**
Reality: A professional with top-university credentials, US work experience, and international network has elite positioning in most home markets. The failure framing comes from peer groups who are also afraid.
Implication: Recalibrate — the question is which path compounds more value, not whether returning is defeat.

**Belief: "Congress will fix the green card backlog."**
Reality: Visa recapture and country-neutral bills have been debated for over a decade without passage. The backlog has grown during this period.
Implication: Model legislative relief as low-probability upside, never as a planning assumption.

**Belief: "Working hard will eventually break the US leadership ceiling."**
Reality: Many senior leadership roles remain structurally harder to access for non-native English speakers without deep US cultural roots. This is a structural ceiling, not a skill gap.
Implication: Assess leadership composition of your organization/industry before assuming the US ceiling matches compensation benchmarks.

**Belief: "Repatriation distress is worst immediately after return."**
Reality: Distress peaks 6–12 months post-return, after the honeymoon phase — not months 1–3.
Implication: Don't evaluate whether the return was right during months 1–3 (falsely positive) or months 6–12 (falsely negative). Suspend judgment until month 12+.

## How to Use This Skill

When a user brings a stay-or-return question: (1) run the diagnostic framework — establish nationality, priority date, actual liquid savings, family situation, and partner viability before anything else; (2) classify which situation type you're actually looking at, since the Indian EB-2/EB-3 trap, fear-of-failure rationalization, and identity-driven return each require different responses; (3) apply the relevant heuristics with their conditions and reasons; (4) surface the specific hidden tradeoffs their situation carries; (5) flag any failure patterns you detect and name them directly. Do not give generic pros-and-cons lists. Do not accept vague future conditions as plans. If the user has already emotionally decided, help them execute cleanly rather than debate the direction — but only after verifying it isn't fear or sunk cost driving the choice.
