WHY THIS IS OFTEN THE ACTUAL BINDING CONSTRAINT

In practice, the single most common reason a stay-or-return decision is harder than expected is a partner whose situation doesn't align. The skill or immigration framework, the financial model, and the career analysis may all point one direction — but the partner's career, visa status, or preferences override everything.

This isn't a secondary consideration. For many people, it IS the decision.

===

THE FUNDAMENTAL ASYMMETRY: DUAL-CAREER vs. SINGLE-EARNER COUPLES

Research finding: Dual-earner couples are materially less geographically mobile than single-earner couples. The reason is structural — relocating requires both partners to simultaneously find acceptable outcomes in the destination.

In the stay-or-return context, this means:
- Single earner (one working, one not): Decision is primarily about the earner's career and family logistics
- Dual earner with similar career trajectories: Both careers must survive the transition — return economics must work for BOTH
- Dual earner with unequal H-1B status: The partner on H-1B/H-4 has DIFFERENT immigration risks than the primary earner on an independent visa

===

COMMON DUAL-CAREER CONFIGURATIONS IN THE US-TO-CHINA/INDIA DECISION

Configuration A: Both are Chinese/Indian nationals on H-1B or green card path
- Both face the same immigration precarity (or one does while the other is a citizen)
- Return economics must be assessed for both careers independently
- Return city options constrained by what's available for BOTH fields (e.g., tech + finance is easier than tech + academia in many Chinese cities)
- If one partner strongly wants to return and the other doesn't, this becomes a relationship question, not a career optimization question

Configuration B: One partner is a US citizen / green card holder, the other isn't
- US citizen partner has no immigration constraint in the US
- H-1B partner faces all the uncertainty
- Returning means the US citizen partner must either find work in China/India (possible but different job market) or give up their US career continuity
- Often generates significant resentment if the decision is perceived as one person's sacrifice

Configuration C: Partners from different countries
- A Chinese national with an Indian partner (or partner from a third country)
- Return to China is complicated by the partner's immigration status in China
- China's spousal visa (family reunion) exists but employment rights for the foreign spouse are limited
- This scenario often leads to third-country options (Singapore, Canada) as genuine compromises

===

WHAT RESEARCH SHOWS ABOUT THE TRAILING PARTNER EFFECT

Documented research finding: "At least one of the partners often suffers career interruptions after relocation, a decline in occupational status, or even career relinquishment."

The trailing partner (the one who didn't drive the relocation decision) faces:
- Starting a job search from scratch in an unfamiliar market
- Credential misalignment (degrees and work history valued differently)
- Network gap — all professional contacts are in the US
- Possible visa/work authorization issues if they're a foreign national in the destination country
- Income gap during job search transition (often 3–12 months)

This effect is asymmetric by gender: research shows women are significantly more likely to be the trailing partner in international relocation decisions, with larger career interruptions.

===

THE "TAKE TURNS" COMPROMISE

Some dual-career couples manage the decision by agreeing to take turns being the one who subordinates their career:
- Phase 1 (2–3 years): Return to home country for one partner's career/family priority
- Phase 2: Evaluate and potentially re-emigrate to support the other partner's opportunity

This requires:
- Explicit agreement upfront about timing and conditions (not implicit understanding)
- Financial planning for the lower-income phase
- Realistic assessment of whether the "return phase" career can be paused and restarted

The failure mode: "Taking turns" gets announced but never actually implemented — the phase 2 re-emigration gets deferred indefinitely, generating resentment in the partner who "sacrificed."

===

THE SPLIT-FAMILY SCENARIO

When relocation doesn't work for both partners simultaneously:
- One partner returns; the other stays in the US temporarily (months to years)
- Common for: kids in school mid-year, one partner with unvested equity, one partner mid-deal or mid-project
- "Temporarily" often extends — research shows split-family arrangements rarely self-resolve on the planned timeline

True cost of split-family: Beyond the financial cost of maintaining two households (easily $5,000–$10,000/month), research shows relationship quality declines significantly in split-family arrangements sustained beyond 6 months.

===

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTIONS FOR DUAL-CAREER SITUATIONS

For the user considering return:
1. "What does your partner actually want to do, in their own terms?" (Not "they're supportive" — what do THEY want?)
2. "What is your partner's career trajectory if you return? Is there a viable path?"
3. "What is your partner's immigration status in the destination country if they're foreign-born?"
4. "Have you two actually planned what the first 6 months look like for both of you, separately?"
5. "What happens to the relationship if the trailing partner's career doesn't work out as hoped?"
6. "Who absorbs the income loss during transition, and for how long?"

Red flag: A user who has clearly modeled their own career return economics in detail but hasn't talked specifically with their partner about what returning means for the partner's career trajectory.

===

THE VISA COMPLICATION LAYER

If one partner is a US citizen or green card holder and the other is an international national:
- In China: Foreign nationals can get family reunion visas, but work authorization is separate and not guaranteed. Work permits require employer sponsorship from a Chinese employer.
- In India: Indian spouse visa (OCI for Indian nationals with foreign spouses) gives residency rights but employment still requires work authorization through employer
- In Singapore/Canada (third options): Both partners get a cleaner immigration pathway; this is why Singapore is a popular compromise choice

The immigration asymmetry in dual-career cases often makes third-country options genuinely more viable than either partner's home country.
