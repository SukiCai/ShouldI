THE CORE PROBLEM: INVISIBLE TAX COSTS OF LEAVING CANADA

Immigrants who become Canadian PR or citizens and later return home face a set of Canadian tax consequences that are less well-known than the equivalent US exit tax situation. The Canadian rules are different — and in some ways less punishing — but ignoring them can be expensive.

Key difference from US: Canada does NOT have ongoing worldwide taxation of permanent residents after they leave. Once you are no longer a Canadian resident for tax purposes, Canada stops taxing your foreign income. There is, however, a "deemed disposition" (departure tax) on most assets at the time of leaving.

===

DETERMINING YOUR CANADIAN RESIDENT STATUS FOR TAX PURPOSES

Canada's Income Tax Act uses a "residential ties" test, not a fixed day count:

Primary residential ties (most important):
- Home in Canada (owned or leased)
- Spouse or common-law partner in Canada
- Dependents in Canada

Secondary residential ties:
- Personal property in Canada (vehicle, furniture)
- Social ties (memberships, church, community)
- Economic ties (employment, bank accounts, RRSP)
- Provincial health insurance card
- Driver's license
- Canadian passport (note: citizenship ≠ tax residency)

If you have primary residential ties with another country AND have severed Canadian ties: You're a non-resident for Canadian tax from the date you leave.

If you maintain a home or family in Canada while working abroad: CRA may consider you still a Canadian resident for tax purposes — this is the "extended stay overseas" trap.

===

DEPARTURE TAX: THE DEEMED DISPOSITION RULE

When you become a Canadian non-resident, you are deemed to have disposed of most of your property at fair market value on the date of departure.

This applies to:
- Shares, ETFs, mutual funds, and investment portfolios (non-registered accounts)
- Investment property (secondary real estate)
- Business assets held personally
- Certain trust interests

Capital gain calculation:
- Gain = Fair Market Value on departure date − Adjusted Cost Base (ACB)
- This gain is reported on the final departure year Canadian tax return
- Taxed at 50% inclusion rate (capital gains inclusion rate) at your marginal rate

Exclusions (property NOT subject to deemed disposition):
- RRSP / RRIF (treated separately — see below)
- Canadian real estate (not subject to departure tax, but subject to non-resident withholding when eventually sold)
- Canadian pension plans
- Shares of a Canadian private corporation in some circumstances
- Personal-use property under $10,000

Practical scale for a Canadian tech professional leaving after 5 years:
- Typical non-registered investment portfolio: $30,000–$80,000 CAD
- Unrealized gains if started from zero 5 years ago: $5,000–$25,000 (depending on contributions and market)
- Tax on deemed disposition: $2,500–$12,000 approximately
- Not catastrophic, but must be planned for — especially if you have significant non-registered investments

===

RRSP: THE MOST IMPORTANT CANADIAN ACCOUNT TO UNDERSTAND

Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP):
- RRSP is EXCLUDED from departure tax — no deemed disposition on departure
- However, once you are a non-resident: withdrawals face non-resident withholding tax

Non-resident RRSP withholding rates:
- Default: 25% flat withholding on each withdrawal
- Tax treaty reduced rates:
  - Canada-India treaty: 25% (same as default; no reduction)
  - Canada-China treaty: 25% (same as default; no reduction)

Note: Canada's treaties with India and China do NOT reduce withholding on periodic RRSP withdrawals. Compare to the US-India treaty (which reduces withholding on pension distributions to potentially 15% or lower).

Unlike 401k, RRSP withdrawals are considered ordinary income — NOT subject to early withdrawal penalty equivalent in Canada. However, as a non-resident, the 25% withholding is final tax for most treaty situations.

Strategy for RRSP before leaving:
- Do NOT over-contribute in final Canadian years if you plan to leave
- Consider making withdrawals while still a Canadian resident (taxed at marginal rate — may be lower if you leave partway through year and have limited income)
- A "final-year strategy": If your departure date is mid-year, withdraw from RRSP before leaving to be taxed at a lower marginal rate vs. the flat 25% non-resident withholding

RRSP Home Buyers' Plan: If you withdrew from RRSP under HBP (up to $35,000 for first home), repayment obligation continues even after you leave Canada. Failure to repay adds the amount to your income each year.

RRSP Lifelong Learning Plan: Similar repayment obligation post-departure.

===

TFSA (TAX-FREE SAVINGS ACCOUNT): LEAVING CANADA

TFSA contributions in Canada grow tax-free. On departure:
- No deemed disposition for TFSA at departure
- BUT: As a non-resident, you can no longer contribute to a TFSA
- If you contribute to TFSA while a non-resident: 1% per month penalty on contributions

What happens to TFSA while you're non-resident:
- Existing investments continue in the account
- No more room accumulates while non-resident
- Withdrawals face non-resident withholding tax in some cases (check with advisor — there is nuance by account type)

General guidance: If leaving Canada, stop contributing to TFSA on departure date. Leave existing investments in place unless you need the cash or are concerned about future Canadian withholding.

===

CANADIAN REAL ESTATE: NON-RESIDENT SELLER RULES

If you own Canadian real estate and later sell it as a non-resident (PR or citizen):

Non-resident withholding on sale (Section 116 of Income Tax Act):
- Buyer must withhold 25% of gross sale price (NOT profit — the full sale price) as a security deposit
- Seller must file a Section 116 clearance certificate before or after closing
- Without clearance certificate, buyer is personally liable for the tax

Procedure:
1. Notify CRA within 10 days of sale
2. File Section 116 clearance certificate application
3. Pay estimated tax on capital gain
4. CRA issues clearance certificate
5. Buyer releases withheld funds minus actual tax

Example: Selling a Toronto condo for $800,000 as a non-resident.
- Withholding: 25% × $800,000 = $200,000 held by buyer
- If you paid $550,000 (ACB), capital gain = $250,000; taxable gain = $125,000 (50% inclusion); tax ~= $50,000–$60,000
- After clearance cert, you get back $200,000 − $55,000 = ~$145,000

The key risk: Sellers often underestimate the cash flow impact of the $200,000 withholding sitting with the buyer for months while clearance cert processing occurs. Plan for this delay.

===

FILING OBLIGATIONS AFTER LEAVING CANADA

Year of departure:
- File a Canadian tax return for the year of departure (as a departing resident)
- Report worldwide income for the period you were a resident
- Report deemed disposition gains
- File by April 30 of following year (same as regular deadline)

After departure (non-resident):
- File Form T1159 (Income Tax Return for Electing Under Section 216) only if you have Canadian rental income
- File non-resident tax returns for Canadian-source income (dividends, rent) if applicable
- No obligation to report foreign income

CPP (Canada Pension Plan):
- If you contributed to CPP, you may be entitled to CPP retirement pension even as a non-resident
- Apply through Service Canada; payments can be made to a foreign bank account
- Note: CPP payments are subject to 25% non-resident withholding (reduced to 15% under some treaties)

===

PRACTICAL SUMMARY: WHAT TO DO BEFORE LEAVING CANADA

1. If you have non-registered investments: Calculate unrealized gains and consider timing of disposition before vs. after departure — sometimes realizing gains before leaving (when full Canadian tax applies) is better than the deemed disposition route if your income is low in the departure year

2. RRSP: Consider partial withdrawal before departure if your effective rate while departing is lower than 25%; otherwise, leave in place and manage timing of withdrawals as non-resident

3. TFSA: Stop contributing on departure date; leave existing balance in place

4. Real estate: If planning to sell, sell before departure (cleaner process) or budget for Section 116 withholding and clearance cert delays

5. CPP: Keep track of your Social Insurance Number — you'll need it to eventually claim CPP

6. CRA notification: No mandatory form to notify CRA you've left, but include departure date on final tax return. Consider filing a letter to CRA if you want a definitive "non-resident" designation to avoid issues with employers still reporting Canadian income

7. Provincial health insurance: Cancel provincial health coverage on departure (each province has different rules; most require notification and coverage ends)
