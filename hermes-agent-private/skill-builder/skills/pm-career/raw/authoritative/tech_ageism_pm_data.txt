EEOC DATA: STRUCTURAL AGE BIAS IN TECH (2024 REPORT)

Key finding: Workers over 40 made up 56% of the US high-tech workforce in 2014. By 2022, that had shrunk to 52% — a 4-percentage-point decline in 8 years, counter to overall workforce aging trends.

By comparison, 25–35-year-olds represent:
- 41% of the tech workforce
- Only 33% of the broader US workforce

Implication: Tech companies structurally over-hire young workers relative to the general labor market.

Age discrimination complaint rate by sector (EEOC, 2022):
- Tech sector: 19.8% of complaints involved age discrimination
- All other industries: 14.8% average

Age-related discrimination complaints in tech are more prevalent than in any other sector except one — tech joins construction as the industries with highest rates of age-based complaints relative to total complaint volume.

===

THE "35 IS OLD" THRESHOLD

Research finding: Tech workers over 35 are already considered "old" in the industry.

Empirical observations:
- Startup leaders actively seek talent with 5–7 years of experience — enough to be independently productive but not enough to be "rigid"
- One documented pattern: startup founders not looking for "old talent" — defining "old" as 35+
- 35% of tech workers report being considered "too old" for their job
- 32% worry about losing their role due to age

This is not just perception: the EEOC data supports an actual employment shrinkage for 40+ workers in the high-tech sector.

===

AGE BIAS PATTERNS BY CAREER STAGE

Against workers under 25 (recent trend, 2023–2025):
- Gen Z representation at large public tech firms dropped from 15% to under 7% between January 2023 and August 2025
- Average workforce age rose from 34.3 to 39.4 at these firms in the same period
- Software developers ages 22–25 saw a 20% employment drop between October 2022 and September 2025
- Reason: companies prefer "5–7 years experience" sweet spot over entry-level

Against workers over 40:
- Perceived as inflexible, unable to "move fast and break things"
- Stereotyped as lacking stamina for long hours
- Assumed to have family obligations that preclude full commitment
- Silicon Valley's biologically-indexed performance culture reinforces these biases

The squeeze is real in both directions: 25-year-olds and 42-year-olds both face more hiring friction than 30–35-year-olds.

===

IMPLICATIONS FOR PM CAREER PLANNING

For PMs currently in their early 30s:
- The 30–37 range is the peak hirability window for senior IC and director roles in tech
- Use this window to build credentials, network, and optionality
- Waiting until 40 to pursue founding, VC, or other pivots may face institutional bias; building the optionality earlier reduces this exposure

For PMs approaching or past 35:
- The job search takes materially longer (not impossible, but plan for it)
- Target companies where age diversity is structurally valued: enterprise tech, regulated industries (fintech, healthtech), European-linked companies, and non-consumer tech sectors
- Consulting and advisory roles become more accessible (not penalized by age in the same way)
- Startups are a harder market than large companies for 40+ PMs unless you have domain expertise the startup specifically needs

For PMs at 40+:
- "PM is a young person's career" is a documented bias, not a ceiling — but it is a real market friction
- Large established companies (Microsoft, Salesforce, Oracle, SAP, enterprise software) have more age-diverse PM teams than consumer tech companies
- Government and civic tech PM roles are not subject to the same age skew
- The PM→VC, PM→advisory, PM→consulting pivots all face less age discrimination than direct PM hiring

===

HOW AGE DISCRIMINATION MANIFESTS IN HIRING (QUALITATIVE DATA)

Common patterns reported by 40+ job seekers:
1. "Culture fit" rejections that correlate with age without explicit mention
2. Being interviewed by hiring managers who are 5–10 years younger and visibly uncomfortable with authority questions
3. Offers at lower levels than experience would warrant (downlevel + lowball comp combo)
4. Long gaps between interview rounds that resolve in ghosting rather than rejection
5. "Overqualified" signals that mean "we think you'll leave or cost too much"

Structural causes:
- ATS (applicant tracking systems) that filter by graduation year or year-count of experience
- Interview loops staffed by junior engineers who may bias toward familiar candidate profiles
- Hiring committees that weight "culture fit" without defining it, creating a proxy discrimination mechanism

===

THE LEGAL PROTECTION GAP

Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) protects workers 40+ from discrimination in hiring, promotion, compensation, and termination. However:
- Proving age discrimination in a hiring decision is extremely difficult (companies don't document the reason)
- Settlement data shows large tech companies have paid out ADEA claims but have not faced systemic reform
- The EEOC complaint process is slow and typically results in settlements, not published verdicts

Practical implication: Legal protection exists on paper but provides limited practical recourse during a job search. Prevention (building strong network, strong credentials before 40) is more effective than legal remedy after the fact.

===

COUNTER-EVIDENCE AND NUANCE

Not all sectors or companies are equally biased:
- Enterprise software PM roles often explicitly value "gray hair" — customer relationships and domain depth in regulated industries (finance, healthcare, logistics) are hard to develop quickly
- PM roles at companies where the customer base is 35–55 years old benefit from PM teams who understand those customers
- Large-company management tracks (Director, VP) are less affected by age bias than IC roles — PM leaders at 45–55 are common; senior IC PMs at 50+ are rare in consumer tech

The data supports: age bias in tech hiring is real, documented, and concentrated in entry-to-senior IC roles at consumer tech companies. It is less severe for management track, enterprise tech, and non-consumer industries.
