THE COMPENSATION GAP (AND THE HIDDEN COSTS)

Base salary: Early-stage startups typically offer 30–40% lower base salaries than Big Tech for equivalent roles.

Equity offset (on paper): 0.5–2% of company equity is typical startup compensation.

Why the equity math doesn't work out as advertised:
- ~90% of startups fail completely, making options worthless
- A 1% stake can become 0.3% even if the company grows (dilution through funding rounds)
- Liquidation preferences reduce actual payouts by 60–80%
- Options often require purchase within 90 days of departure, costing 20–50% of annual salary just to preserve them

Big Tech compensation structure:
- Base + annual bonus of 10–20%
- Stock grants vest over 4 years and are immediately sellable
- 30–50% additional value from stock refreshers and promotions
- Predictable and liquid — you know what you have

The right test: "Subtract equity entirely and assess whether remaining compensation meets living expenses." If not, you're gambling with money you need.

===

SCOPE AND IMPACT: THE REAL TRADEOFF

Startup advantages:
- Your work directly moves the needle on company success
- Ship features that affect key metrics and make architectural decisions that shape the entire product
- Direct mentor access to founders and senior operators
- Breadth of experience in how businesses actually operate
- Visible individual contribution impact — you can point to exactly what you shipped

Big Tech advantages:
- Serve millions of users; scale of impact is larger in aggregate
- Access to best-in-class tools and formal training startups can't provide
- Deep expertise in established product frameworks
- Colleagues are often the best in their field

The tension: Big Tech impact is more diffuse — your individual contribution "gets lost in the machine." Startup impact is visible but at smaller scale (unless you hit the 1-in-10 that succeeds).

===

TITLE INFLATION VS. REAL SCOPE

Critical insight: Title ≠ scope at either end.

Startups hand out Director and VP titles because they can't compete on comp. Big Tech has so many leveling bands that "Senior" means wildly different things at different orgs.

What actually matters:
1. What will you own? (decision surface)
2. What decisions will you make? (autonomy)
3. Who will you influence? (organizational reach)

"Those three questions tell you more about a role than the title ever could."

===

RESUME IMPLICATIONS

Startup success → opens doors everywhere: you're a founder-type who shipped under constraints
Startup failure → attractive to other startups but less so to Big Tech
Big Tech experience → opens doors everywhere, but can pigeonhole you into specific tech stacks
Big Tech → startup → most credible path for senior leadership roles at growth-stage companies

Counter-intuitive reality: If you're early in your career, startup experience at a failed company with meaningful scope still teaches more than 2 years as a mid-level PM in a large Big Tech team.

===

GROWTH AND LEARNING TRAJECTORIES

Early career (0–5 years): Prioritize learning velocity over everything else.
- Startup: Immediate exposure to CI/CD, database performance, customer escalations, and how decisions get made under uncertainty
- Big Tech: Formal training, exposure to product at scale, but often more narrow initial scope

Mid-career (5–10 years): Learning plateaus in Big Tech are real; startup scope can accelerate trajectory.

Late career (10+ years): Startup → Big Tech transition becomes harder; Big Tech credentialism matters more to employers for senior hires.

===

RISK PROFILE CHANGES WITH LIFE STAGE

The risk tolerance calculation changes completely with financial obligations:
- Early career, no mortgage, no dependents: startup risk is manageable
- With mortgage and family: startup runway failure means real financial exposure
- "Risk tolerance evolves, and this is appropriate — matching risk to life stage is rational, not timid"

Life stage context is the variable most PM career advice ignores when comparing startup vs. Big Tech.

===

DUE DILIGENCE CHECKLIST

For startup offers:
- Monthly recurring revenue and customer churn rates (ask directly)
- Burn rate and runway until next funding round
- Path to profitability and contingencies if funding fails
- Liquidation preferences on existing rounds (affects your equity payout order)
- Exercise window length (standard 90 days vs. extended 5-year windows are very different)

Red flags: "Talks more about vision than customers" or can't give specific runway numbers.

For Big Tech offers:
- Team's recent shipping record (last 3 products shipped)
- Manager quality and decision-making style
- Promotion trajectory (time-to-promo at your level in this org)
- Team roadmap clarity

Red flags: High turnover, vague job descriptions, managers who "can't explain why the role exists."

===

DECISION FRAMEWORK

5-year vision test: Define where you want to be in 5 years — specific skills, network, and financial position. Then evaluate which path aligns better.

Regret minimization: "Which choice will you regret more at 80?" (Bezos framework)

Core principle: "Optimize for your next two jobs, not your entire career or just this year's salary." Neither path is wrong if you commit fully — half-hearted commitment produces poor outcomes in either environment.

Set a 1-week information-gathering deadline, then decide and commit.

===

THE HIDDEN ASYMMETRY

Big Tech → startup is easy and always available. Startup → Big Tech requires either startup success (proving yourself) or strong credentials (school, prior big-tech experience).

Implication for sequencing: If you want both experiences, doing Big Tech first is lower-risk — you preserve optionality in both directions. Starting at a startup narrows your Big Tech entry options unless the startup succeeds.
