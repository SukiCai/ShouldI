  This is a genuinely strong skill that captures how a seasoned PM/career advisor thinks about the four look-alike situations that map to opposite answers. It stands out for its diagnostic-first structure, refusal to conflate scope/title/comp, and specific data points (levels, comp bands, HBS placement stats). Weaknesses are minor: some heuristics still feel like well-articulated common wisdom, and it under-covers a few situations (post-layoff repositioning, PM→adjacent role pivots, dual-career/relocation constraints).

  ✓ Strengths:
    • The four-way classification (skill gap / org ceiling / visibility gap / wrong track) is genuinely useful — it names the specific misdiagnosis pattern that a novice advisor would miss, and each has distinguishing signals (e.g., 'consistent, specific, evolving feedback' vs 'recycled feedback, no headcount at next level'). This is the core of the skill and it earns its keep.
    • Diagnostic Question 3 — 'Can your manager describe your last 6 months of impact specifically and compellingly without your help?' — is an expert-level question that surfaces the visibility gap most PMs don't know exists. Similarly, the market-vs-HR definition of Senior PM and the level code insistence ('Amazon L6 = Google/Meta Senior PM, not L5') are concrete, non-obvious, and immediately usable.
    • The Junior vs. Senior table has real differentiators, not fluff. 'Shipped vs. Worked' as the definition of success, and 'Subtracts equity entirely; models dilution, liquidation prefs, exercise cost' are the kind of moves that actually separate senior from junior thinking.

  ✗ Weaknesses:
    • Heuristic #10 ('Optimize for your next two jobs, not your entire career or just this year's comp') gestures at a good idea but doesn't operationalize it — what does 'sequencing' actually look like? A junior reader gets the vibe but not the move. Contrast with #9 which is fully concrete.
    • Some 'Industry Realities' pairs are strong (MBA post-2022 with HBS stat) but others state a belief that isn't really widespread among senior PMs — e.g., 'Strong performance naturally leads to promotion' is more of a mid-level naiveté; framing it as a 'belief' feels like a straw man for the target user of this skill.
    • The failure patterns are good but lean heavily on decision-framing patterns; missing are patterns around timing (e.g., leaving right before a re-org that would have changed everything, or accepting an internal transfer that resets the promo clock silently), which are common real-world PM career mistakes.
    • Comp figures ('Intuit Principal PM median (~$482K)') are presented as facts without dates — these numbers drift fast and the skill will age badly without a 'verify against levels.fyi at time of use' caveat.

  → Improvement Suggestions:
    • Add a fifth diagnostic situation: 'Wrong company stage' — PM at hypergrowth co whose skills are strong but company has entered maintenance mode, or vice versa. This is distinct from org ceiling (there IS headcount, but the work has changed) and shows up frequently.
    • Operationalize heuristic #10 with an actual model — e.g., 'For each option, write the plausible next role in 2-3 years. If the next role from Option A is the current role from Option B, you're taking a detour.' This transforms it from a slogan into a tool.
    • Add explicit coverage for: (a) internal transfer vs. external move tradeoffs (promo clock reset, tenure signal, network cost), (b) post-layoff repositioning where the diagnostic framework needs adjustment because leverage is different, and (c) the PM→adjacent-role pivot (CoS, PMM, founding) as more than a bullet in activation criteria.
    • Date-stamp comp figures and add a heuristic: 'Never advise on comp bands without pulling current levels.fyi data — figures in this skill are directional as of [date] and comp bands shift materially year-over-year.'
    • Consider adding a 'What to say to your manager' section — concrete scripts for the 'here's the challenge, here's my proposed approach' pattern and the promo-gap conversation. The skill names these as leverage points but doesn't show them in action.

  ⚠ Generic Advice Flags:
    • 'Optimize for your next two jobs, not your entire career or just this year's comp' — directionally right but as written this is close to a career-advice cliché without a mechanism. What's the actual test?
    • 'Because: executives cannot calibrate trust in reasoning they cannot observe' — true but stated at a level of abstraction that most leadership books also state. Would benefit from a specific example of what 'showing alternatives you considered' looks like in a PM review doc.
    • 'Treats moves as hypotheses tested through small experiments' (in the Senior column of the table) — this is a bit aspirational; career moves are rarely reversible experiments. Risks being an experienced-sounding platitude unless paired with what an actual 'small experiment' looks like (e.g., taking on an adjacent-scope side project before committing to a track switch).

  ○ Missing Coverage:
    • Post-layoff or forced-transition scenarios, where the diagnostic (skill gap vs org ceiling) is muddled by the fact that the org made the decision, not the PM.
    • Internal transfer as a distinct option — most PM career questions include 'should I move teams internally?' which has different mechanics than staying or leaving (promo clock, tenure signal, relationship transfer costs).
    • The PM→adjacent role pivot (PMM, CoS, founding, VC, GM) is mentioned in activation criteria but not developed — no diagnostic for 'is this a real pivot or an escape?'
    • Manager quality as an explicit variable — a great manager at a mediocre company often beats a mediocre manager at a great company for career development, and this isn't surfaced.
    • Life-stage / non-career constraints (dual career, relocation, visa, caregiving) that legitimately constrain the optimal move — the skill implicitly assumes a fully mobile, unconstrained PM.
